In this paper, we develop an online alternative to Split Packing to handle an online sequence of insertions and deletions, where the algorithm is allowed to reallocate circles into new positions at a cost proportional to their areas. The algorithm can be used to pack circles into squares and right angled triangles. If only insertions are considered, our algorithm is also able to achieve optimal packing density as defined in our paper, with an amortized reallocation cost of O(c log 1c ) for squares, and O(c(1 + s2 ) log1+s2 1c ) for right angled triangles, where s is the ratio of the lengths of the second shortest side to the shortest, when inserting a circle of area c. When insertions and deletions are considered, we achieve a packing density of (1 − ) of the optimal, where  > 0 can be made arbitrarily small, for an additional amortized reallocation cost of O(c 1 ). Digital Object Identifier 10.4230/LIPIcs.CIRCLELOVERS.2018.23  1  Introduction  A common class of problem in data structures requires handling a sequence of online requests. In a dynamic resource allocation problem, one has to handle a sequence of allocation and deallocation requests. A good allocation algorithm would run fast, and allocate items in a way that uses as few resources as possible to store the items. Classical solutions disallow the algorithm from reallocating already-placed items except during deletion. In our problem, the algorithm is allowed to reallocate already-placed items with additional cost. Instead of bounding the running time of the algorithm, we focus on trying to bound the reallocation costs required to handle the sequence of requests, while minimising the amount of space required to handle all the requests. In this paper, we look at online circle packing, where we try to dynamically pack a set of circles of unequal areas into a unit square while allowing for reallocations. Our work builds on insights from the Split Packing paper by Fekete, Morr, and Scheffer [14, 24]. The Split Packing paper derives the optimal packing density a of squares and obtuse triangles (triangles with an internal angle of at least 90◦ ). The optimal packing density of a region is the largest value a such that any set of circles with total area at most a can be packed into the region. To simplify our discussion, we sometimes refer to the optimal packing density of a region as the “capacity” of the region. As shown in the paper [14, 24], the optimal packing density for squares is equal to the combined area of the two circles in the configuration shown in Figure 3a. The optimal packing density for obtuse triangles is the incircle area of the triangle. An allocation algorithm is said to have a packing density of A if it is able to handle any sequence of insertion and deletion requests, as long as the total area of the circles in the region at any point of time is at most A. The algorithm achieves optimal packing density on a region if it attains a packing density equal to the region’s capacity. To obtain good bounds on the relative reallocation costs in the online setting, one may need to pack to less than optimal packing density to allow for sufficient space to move circles. In our model, the cost of reallocating a circle is proportional to the area of the circle itself (volume cost). Note that insertion and deletion costs are out of the algorithm’s control. We do not require the reallocations to be in place. This means that when a set of circles needs to be reallocated to new positions, we can see all of them as being simultaneously removed from the packing, then re-added in their new locations. (This is in contrast to requiring the circles to be moved one at a time to their new locations.)  1.2  Results  Our main result is the Online Split Packing Algorithm, an online circle packing algorithm based on the original Split Packing Algorithm. The Online Split Packing Algorithm can be used to pack circles into squares and right angled triangles. 1. For insertions only into a square, the algorithm achieves optimal packing density, with an amortised reallocation cost of O(c(log2 1c )) for inserting a circle of area c.√ 2. Consequently, we add an amortised reallocation cost of O(c 1 ) for inserting a circle of area c. Gilbert  1.3  23:3  Challenges of Online Circle Packing  The problem of packing circles into a square is a difficult problem in general. The decision problem of whether a set of circles of possibly unequal areas can be packed into a square has been shown to be NP-HARD [9]. Given a set of n equal circles, the problem of finding the smallest square that can fit these circles only has proven optimal solutions for n ≤ 35 [23]. Packing a square to optimal packing density has only been recently solved by the Split Packing paper in 2017. The arrangement of circles in a tight packing is highly dependent on the size distribution of the circles. Small differences in circle sizes can cause large variations in the required arrangement of circles. The Split Packing Algorithm achieves optimal packing density by packing circles in a top-down, divide and conquer fashion that starts with sorting the initial set of circles by size and partitioning the circles. Small changes to the inputs result in very different packings. In an online setting, however, there is a need to be able to repack only a part of the configuration at a time. With insertions and deletions, larger circles may be replaced with smaller circles within a subregion of the original shape, which may cause problems as if the subregion can no longer support that set of circles. The Online Split Packing Algorithm is able to pack circles tightly while being able to handle an sequence of circles with reallocations. It does this by making use of a binary tree structure similar to the original Split Packing Algorithm. By designing a new packing strategy, we are able to maintain a tree where left children are packed tightly, while extra slack is kept in the rightmost child of each level to allow for circle movement. New circles are recursively inserted into the rightmost child of each level, where it eventually triggers a repack of an entire node somewhere down the tree to allocate the circle.  1.4  Related Work  There is a large amount of existing work for online resource allocation problems that do not allow items to be reallocated once placed. For example, online square packing into squares has been studied by Han et al. [17] and Fekete and Hoffman [12]. See also similar online packing problems, like squares in unbounded strips [13], packing of rectangles [1] and multiple variants of online bin packing [8, 10, 11, 21, 27]. While resource allocation problems that allow for reallocations are less commonly studied, there is existing work that employs similar models to achieve better bounds than if reallocations were not permitted. Ivković [20] studies dynamic resource allocation for bin packing. They provide an algorithm that is 54 -competitive with the best practical offline algorithms, with a running time of θ(log n) when reallocations are allowed. This beats the lower bound of 43 on the competitive ratio if reallocations are not considered. Berndt [7] studies this problem further and achieves better upper and lower bounds. There is also work that focuses on bounding the reallocation cost from reallocating items rather than algorithm running times. A closely related work to our own is online square packing with reallocations by Fekete et al. [15]. Bender et al. studies dynamic resource allocation with reallocation costs in problems like scheduling [4], memory allocation [3] and maintaining modules on an FPGA [5]. Our work also takes ideas from packed memory arrays [2, 6]. There has been plenty of work on circle packing. Most existing work focuses on the global, offline optimisation problem of packing either equal or unequal circles into various shapes. Many existing results for circle packing are collected in the packomania website [25]. Many heuristic methods have also been developed for offline circle packing. Given a square or obtuse triangle as the region, and any set of circles with total area at most the capacity of the region, the Split Packing Algorithm can pack the set of circles into the region. The algorithm works in a top down, divide-and-conquer fashion. In the simple case, if the Split Packing Algorithm is given a single circle to be packed into a region, the circle is simply placed into the center of the region. If given at least two circles to be packed, the algorithm partitions the circles into two sets, splits the region into two smaller subregions, and recursively packs each set of circles into its corresponding subregion. The Split Packing paper describes how a region of capacity a is to be split into two smaller regions of capacities a1 and a2 respectively, where a1 + a2 ≤ a. If the original region is an obtuse triangle, a is its incircle area. As shown in Figure 2b, the two subregions will be right angled triangles, defined by squeezing circles of areas a1 and a2 respectively into the left and right corners of the original triangle, which has been oriented such that the obtuse angle is at the top. If the original region is a square, a is as shown in Figure 3a. As shown in Figure 3b, the two subregions will be isosceles right angled triangles, defined by squeezing circles of areas a1 and a2 respectively into opposite corners of the square. In both cases, the capacities of the two subregions will then be a1 and a2 respectively. The Split Packing paper proves that as long as a1 + a2 ≤ a, the two subregions will not overlap each other. Note that the two subregions may not be contained within the original region, as shown in Figures 8 and 9. To account for this, Split Packing rounds the corners of triangles to form hats, which will remain within the bounds of their parent regions. Our algorithm relies on insights from the Split Packing Algorithm for packing circles into a square. The Split Packing Algorithm has a binary tree structure, where each shape is recursively split into two smaller shapes. We follow a similar structure in our Online Circle Packing Algorithm. The root node is either a square or a right angled triangle. A square splits into two right angled triangles,  CIRCLELOVERS  23:6  A Reallocation Algorithm for Online Split Packing of Circles  and a right angled triangle splits into two smaller right angled triangles. We also use similar definitions to those used by the Split Packing Algorithm. We first discuss a version of the algorithm that only handles insertions. Deletions will be discussed in Section 7. A newly-inserted circle goes into the root of the binary tree, and is recursively inserted into the right child of each subsequent node, until a repack is triggered at some level. A repack completely repacks all the circles in the node and its descendants. The intuition behind our algorithm is that when a shape is split into two smaller shapes, the left child of the shape will be packed tightly using the original Split Packing Algorithm, while the right child is packed with extra room using the Online Circle Packing Algorithm. Repacks shift circles towards the left children, which are then tightly packed by the Split Packing Algorithm. This allows us to obtain a tight packing of circles equivalent to the Split Packing Algorithm. For squares and isosceles right angled triangles, we have a relative amortised cost of O(log 1c + 1 ) when inserting a circle of size c. The bound is asymptotically the same for arbitrary right angled triangles, if we treat the ratio of the lengths of the shortest two sides of the triangle as a constant. We note however, that the Split Packing Algorithm has been generalised further than right angled triangles, as it has been shown to work for any obtuse triangle (any triangle with an interior angle of at least 90◦ ). Initial attempts to generalise the Online Split Packing Algorithm to obtuse triangles failed. It is currently unknown whether the same results can be applied to obtuse right angled triangles. In addition to packing circles into a square, the Split Packing algorithm has also been shown to work for packing many similar shapes, like squares and octagons, into triangles. The paper goes on to define a new type of shape, a “Gem”, which represents the most general type of shape that the Split Packing Algorithm can handle. As the Online Split Packing Algorithm builds on the Split Packing Algorithm, it is possible that a similar type of generalisation could be applied to the Online Split Packing Algorithm.  S. Oh and S. Gilbert  23:17  We note that the deletion procedure defined in Section 7 works for any tight packing algorithm for insertions only into a fixed space. The deletion procedure can also be shown to work not only for tight packing algorithms, but also for packing algorithms that achieve packing densities that are arbitrarily close to the optimal. The current reallocation cost bounds apply only to volume costs. Another possible direction of future work would be to understand how the cost bounds differ for the other types of cost models like unit cost (constant cost for each circle reallocation), or to see how the algorithm can be adapted to work with other cost models.
