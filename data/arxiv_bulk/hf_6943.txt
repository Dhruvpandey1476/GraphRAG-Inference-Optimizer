arXiv:1611.09641v1 [math.GR] 29 Nov 2016  Involutions of type G2 over a field of characteristic two John Hutchens Winston-Salem State University hutchensjd@wssu.edu Nathaniel Schwartz Washington College nschwartz2@washcoll.edu February 1, 2018  1  Introduction  In this paper we establish a classification of isomorphism classes of automorphisms of order 2 of algebraic groups of the form Aut(C) where C is an octonion algebra defined over a field of characteristic 2 and their fixed point groups. Groups of type G2 were first defined over fields of characteristic 2 by Dickson in [9, 10] and later by Chevalley in [8]. For fields of not characteristic 2, a similar classification has been carried out by Yokota in [26] and Hutchens in [19]. The orginal study of symmetric spaces began with Cartan [5, 6] in 192627, followed by Gantmacher in [14]. Berger also classifies symmetric spaces of real Lie algebras in [3]. Helminck defined a generalization of these real symmetric spaces and called them symmetric k-varieties. He provided a characterization of these spaces in [16, 15] over fields of not characteristic 2. For finite fields this problem was solved by Thomas in [25], and Aschbacher and Seitz included these results to complete their classification of such structures for groups of Lie type over fields of even order in [1]. What we call involutions 1  of type I correspond to Thomas’ central involutions, and our involutions of type II correspond to his non-central involutions over finite fields. Schwartz has provided descriptions for algebraic groups of classical type An over fields of characteristic 2 in [22], and Hutchens has over fields of characteristic not 2 for algebraic groups of type G2 in [19]. In 4.2 we prove the classification of k-involutions depends on isomorphism classes of four dimensional subalgebras of C. This is also true when the characteristic is not 2, which Jacobson proves in [20]. His argument relies on the fact that there is a (−1)-eigenspace for each element of order 2 in Aut(C), the existence of which we forfeit over characteristic 2. Also, four dimensional subalgebras of C are all of quaternion type when the characteristic is not 2. In the characteristic 2 case we have another type of four dimensional subalgebra that corresponds to elements of order 2 in Aut(C). A representative of this second type of involution is used by Callens and de Medts in [4] to construct a class of Moufang sets. Recently there has been a lot interest in topics related to algebras and algebraic groups over characteristic 2, for example [21, 11, 2]. This paper is organized as follows. 2 contains a brief background on composition algebras, including necessary prerequisites. Here, we generally follow the conventions and notations used by Hoffmann and Laghribi in [17]. In 3 we settle on a basis for our octonion algebra C, and we describe two different four dimensional subalgebras: quaternion subalgebras and four-dimensional totally singular subalgebras. The latter is actually a Clifford algebra that only occurs in characteristic 2. We show that every involution in Aut(C) fixes point-wise one of these two types of subalgebras. We call the involutions that fix a quaternion subalgebra type I, and involutions that fix a four-dimensional totally singular subalgebra type II. In 4, we see that there is only one conjugacy class of involutions of type I in the case that the fixed subalgebra is split, and there are no involutions when the fixed subalgebra is a division quaternion subalgebra. We show in 4.11 that the fixed point groups of type I are of the form SL2 (k) × G+ (k), and subsequently describe the fixed point groups of type II. In 5 we describe the involutions in Aut(C) fixing a four dimensional totally singular subalgebra B. We show in 5.9 that these involutions are inb of elements whose norm is equal to the duced by elements in the set B coefficient of the identity component. In 5.10 we show every such involution acts invariantly on B. In 5.22 we establish that, if B is a division algebra, then its automorphism group is trivial. 5.24 summarizes how two involutions 2  can be conjugate when the fixed point algebra is division. We find that all involutions are conjugate to the map u 7→ u + e; hence there is a single conjugacy class of involutions when B is not a division algebra. The entire situation is summarized in 5.27. A short discussion of the corresponding Galois cohomology follows.  2  Preliminaries and composition algebras  If k is a field of characteristic 2, we call k a perfect field if k 2 = k. This is the case for all finite and algebraically closed fields of characteristic 2. For a classification of involutions and their centralizers of Aut(C) over finite fields see [1] and [25]. We denote by Aut(C, B) the subgroup of Aut(C) leaving a subaglebra B ⊂ C invariant and by Aut(C)B the subgroup of Aut(C) leaving B fixed elementwise. The additive group of a field k will be denoted by G+ (k) and the multiplicative group of k is denoted by G∗ (k). When char(k) = 2 the group G+ (k) has subgroups G+ (k 2 ) and ℘(k) = {x2 + x | x ∈ k}. When we consider groups of type G2 over fields of characteristic not 2 they are induced by an element of an automorphism group of an octonion algebra over k that fixes a quaternion subalgebra. We see in [19] that the isomorphism classes of quaternion subalgebras of an octonion algebra C are in bijection with the conjugacy classes of k-involutions of Aut(C). Also, the fixed point group of these k-involutions of Aut(C) have the form Aut(C)It ∼ = Aut(D) ⋉ Sp(1, k), where D is the quaternion subalgebra of C fixed by t ∈ Aut(C) and Sp(1, k) is the group of norm 1 quaternions in D. We have a similar situation in characteristic 2. In 3.2 and 3.3 we show that involutions of Aut(C) in characteristic 2 are induced by elements in Aut(C) that fix four dimensional subalgebras of C, and in 4.11 we see that the fixed point groups take a slightly different form than in the characteristic not 2 case. For the remainder of this paper we will only consider fields of characteristic 2 unless otherwise stated. This is not true when char(k) = 2; there are also totally singular subalgebras with diagnolizable quadratic forms. Along with quaternion subalgebras we are concerned with four dimensional totally singular subalgebras of C, as they correspond to a type of automorphisms of order 2 in Aut(C). When k is not perfect we have octonion division algebras over fields of characteristic 2. In this case their four dimensional subalgebras are all division algebras, and so there are two main classes of of subalgebras: quaternion division algebras and four dimensional totally singular subalgebras. The latter of these are purely separable field extensions of k. We can also have split octonion algebras over non-perfect fields. These algebras have both split and division quaternion subalgebras. Four dimensional totally singular subalgebras can be either split, division, or an intermediate type. In general if we take u ∈ C to be an element such that hu, ei = 1 we can construct a basis for C by taking v, w perpendicular to u and e to get {e, u, v, uv, w, uw, vw, (uv)w}, with a non-singular bilinear form generated by a quadratic form [1, α] ⊥ β[1, α] ⊥ γ([1, α] ⊥ β[1, α]). By taking subsets of this basis that are closed under multiplication we arrive at two main types; the quaternion basis {e, u, v, uv} with a non-singular bilinear form generated by a quadratic form [1, α] ⊥ β[1, α], and the set {e, v, w, vw} with a totally singular bilinear form generated by a quasi-Pfister form hhβ, γii. When k is a perfect field, any octonion algebra is split. In this case the quaternion and four dimensional totally singular subalgebras are all split as well. If k is not perfect, within each of these main types of four dimensional subalgebras there could be many isomorphism classes.
