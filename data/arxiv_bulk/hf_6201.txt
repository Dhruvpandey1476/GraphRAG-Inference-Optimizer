Trees of manifolds as boundaries of spaces and groups  arXiv:1304.5067v2 [math.GR] 4 Mar 2016  Jacek Świa̧tkowski * Abstract. We show that trees of manifolds, the topological spaces introduced by Jakobsche, appear as boundaries at inﬁnity of various spaces and groups. In particular, they appear as Gromov boundaries of some hyperbolic groups, of arbitrary dimension, obtained by the procedure of strict hyperbolization. We also recognize these spaces as boundaries of arbitrary Coxeter groups with manifold nerves, and as Gromov boundaries of the fundamental groups of singular spaces obtained from some ﬁnite volume hyperbolic manifolds by cutting oﬀ their cusps and collapsing the resulting boundary tori to points. MSC Subject Classification: 20F67, 20F65, 57M07. Introduction Not many explicit topological spaces are known to be homeomorphic to Gromov boundaries of hyperbolic groups. The list consists of spheres and Sierpiński compacta of arbitrary dimension, Menger compacta in dimensions d ∈ {1, 2, 3}, Pontriagin sphere and Pontriagin surfaces (which are 2-dimensional), and some trees of 3-manifolds (which are certain spaces of topological dimension 3). See [PS] for more detailed comments concerning this list. One of the goals of this paper is to extend this list to trees of n-manifolds in arbitrary dimension n (for which the methods used in [PS], in the case n = 3, are insuﬃcient). Trees of manifolds have been formally introduced by Wlodzimierz Jakobsche in [J], though their idea goes back to Ancel and Siebenmann [AS]. These are certain explicit homogeneous metric compacta, typically not ANR, appearing in abundance in arbitrary ﬁnite topological dimension. We describe them in detail in Section 1, and here we only mention that each closed connected topological n-manifold M determines uniquely one such space, denoted X (M ), which has topological dimension n, and which we call the tree of manifolds M . Our ﬁrst result is the following (compare Theorem 5.1 in the text, which is slightly more general). Theorem 1. (1) For any natural number n, if a closed connected orientable PL n-manifold M bounds a compact orientable PL (n + 1)-manifold, then the tree of manifolds X (M ) is homeomorphic to the Gromov boundary of some hyperbolic group. (2) For any closed connected non-orientable PL manifold N the tree of manifolds X (N ) is homeomorphic to the Gromov boundary of some hyperbolic group. Appropriate hyperbolic groups as in the assertion of Theorem 1 will be constructed in Section 5, using the procedure of strict hyperbolization due to Charney and Davis [CD]. Trees of manifolds. Trees of manifolds have been introduced by Wlodzimierz Jakobsche in [J]. These are some homogeneous metric compacta, typically not ANR, appearing in abundance in any 2  ﬁnite topological dimension. We brieﬂy recall their description, in the original setting of Jakobsche modiﬁed and extended as in [Sw]. Although the description is valid for arbitrary topological manifolds, in this paper we make use only of the subclass related to PL manifolds. Definiton 1.1. Let M be a ﬁnite family of closed connected n-dimensional topological manifolds, either all oriented, or at least one of which is non-orientable. Let J = ({Xi : i ≥ 1}, {πi : i ≥ 1}) be an inverse sequence consisting of closed connected topological n-manifolds Xi and maps πi : Xi+1 → Xi . Assume furthermore that if all the manifolds in M are oriented then all Xi are also oriented. We say that J is a weak Jakobsche inverse sequence for M if for all i ≥ 1 and all M ∈ M one can choose ﬁnite families Di of collared n-disks in Xi , partitioned into subfamilies DiM : M ∈ M, such that:  (1) for each i ≥ 1 the disks in the family Di are pairwise disjoint; (2) for each i ≥ 1 the map πi maps the preimage πi−1 (Xi \ ∪{int(D) : D ∈ Di }) homeomorphically onto Xi \ ∪{int(D) : D ∈ Di }; (3a) X1 is homeomorphic to one of the manifolds from M, and if the manifolds in M are oriented, we require that this homeomorphism respects orientations; (3b) for each i ≥ 1, for each M ∈ M, and for any D ∈ DiM the preimage πi−1 (D) is homeomorphic to M \ int(∆), where ∆ is some collared n-disk in M ; furthermore, if the manifolds in M are oriented, we require that the above homeomorphism respects the orientations induced from Xi+1 and from M ; (4) if i < j, D ∈ Di , D′ ∈ Dj , then πi,j (D′ )∩∂D = ∅, where πi,j := πi ◦πi+1 ◦. . .◦πj−1 ; (5) for each i ≥ 1 the family {πi,j (D) : j ≥ i, D ∈ Dj } of subsets of Xi is null, i.e. the diameters of these subsets converge to 0; here πi,i denotes the identity map on Xi ; S∞ (6) for any i ≥ 1 and each M ∈ M the set j=i πi,j (∪DjM ) is dense in Xi .  Remarks. (1) It follows from conditions (1), (2), (3a) and (3b) that each Xi is the connected sum of a family of manifolds each homeomorphic to one of the manifolds in M; moreover, if the manifolds in M are oriented, the above mentioned homeomorphisms (and the involved operation of connected sum) respect the orientations. (2) In the case when the manifolds in M are oriented, conditions (1)-(5) in Deﬁnition 1.1 basically coincide with conditions (1)-(6) in [J], Section 2, p. 82. (3) Condition (6) in Deﬁnition 1.1 implies condition (7) in [J], but it is essentially weaker than the conjunction of conditions (7) and (8) of [J] (except when the family M consists of a single manifold M , in which case condition (6) of Deﬁnition 1.1 is equivalent to the conjunction of conditions (7) and (8) of [J], as it was observed and exploited in [F] and [Z]). (4) Deﬁnition 1.1 describes exactly the class of inverse sequences naturally associated to weakly saturated tree systems of manifolds from M, as described in [Sw], Section 3.E. It represents signiﬁcant, and probably close to optimal, relaxation of the initial 3  set of conditions provided by Jakobsche in [J], for which the inverse limit of the corresponding sequence depends uniquely on M (see Theoerem 1.3 below). The following result is a reformulation of Corollary 3.E.4 of [Sw]. Theorem 1.3. Given M as in Definition 1.1, any two weak Jakobsche inverse sequences for M have homeomorphic inverse limits. Definition 1.4. Given M as in Deﬁnition 1.1, denote by X (M), and call the tree of manifolds from M, the space homeomorphic to the inverse limit of some (and hence any) weak Jakobsche inverse sequence for M. Remarks. (1) If M = {M }, we denote the corresponding space X (M) simply by X (M ), and call it the tree of manifolds M . (2) If M = {M1 , . . . , Mk }, it is known that the space X (M) is homeomorphic to the tree of manifolds M1 # . . . #Mk , i.e. to the space X (M1 # . . . #Mk ) (see e.g. Corollary 3.E.4 in [Sw]). As it was shown in [J] and [St], trees of manifolds X (M) are connected homogeneous metric compacta of topological dimension equal to the dimension of the manifolds in M. As it was observed in Corollary 3.3 of [PS], trees of manifolds in dimensions ≥ 2 have no local cut points, and in fact they are Cantor manifolds, i.e. no subsets of topological codimension ≥ 2 separate them. Trees of manifolds X (M) of the same topological dimension n can be sometimes distinguished by means of their homotopical or homological invariants, or the shape theoretic invariants. For example, it is known that if n ≥ 3 then the shape fundamental group π̌1 (X (M)) is isomorphic to the inverse limit of the increasing free products G1 ∗G2 ∗. . .∗Gk , where the additional factors in larger products are collapsed to identity while the common factors are mapped to each other through identities, and where groups Gi are the copies of the fundamental groups π1 (M ) : M ∈ M, each group appearing inﬁnitely often (see [FG]). Obviously, this shape fundamental group is sometimes suﬃcient to distinguish some trees of manifolds. The general question of classifying trees of manifolds up to homeomorphism remains open. 2. (M, κ)-pseudomanifolds and Main Theorem. Let M be a ﬁnite collection of (pairwise distinct) closed connected PL manifolds of the same dimension n, distinct from the standard PL n-sphere, and let κ ∈ {0, −1}. An (M, κ)-pseudomanifold is a metric polyhedral complex X of piecewise constant curvature κ, with ﬁnite shapes, and such that (1) X is CAT (κ); (2) for each point x ∈ X the link Lk(x, X) (viewed as combinatorial polyhedral complex) is either a PL n-sphere, or is PL-homeomorphic to some M ∈ M; furthermore, if all M ∈ M are oriented, we assume that X is also oriented, and that each homeomorphism Lk(x, X) → M as above respects the orientations; 4  (3) for each M ∈ M the set ΛM := {x ∈ X : Lk(x, X) ∼ = M } is a net in X, i.e. for some R > 0 each ball of radius R in X intersects ΛM (if all M ∈ M are oriented, the symbol ∼ = above denotes relation of being PL homeomorphic as oriented manifolds). Note that, since X above has ﬁnite shapes, it is a geodesic metric space, so that it makes sense to speak of the CAT (κ) property for it. Moreover, by condition (2), X is automatically an (n + 1)-dimensional pseudomanifold. The assumption that the link of every point of X is a manifold implies that only the vertices of X can have links that are not spheres. Hence, if x ∈ X and Lk(x, X) ∼ = M ∈ M, than x must be a vertex of X. The ﬁnite shapes property implies that the vertices of X form a discrete set. Therefore, each set ΛM as in condition (3) above is discrete. Putting Λ := ∪M ∈M ΛM , we get that Λ, which will be called the singular set of X, is also discrete. In this section we describe vast classes of examples to which Main Theorem (as presented in Section 2) applies. Among others, we provide proofs of Theorems 1 and 2 from the introduction. Hyperbolizations of M-pseudomanifolds and the proof of Theorem 1.  Given a ﬁnite family M of PL manifolds as in Section 2 (all of the same dimension n), a closed M-pseudomanifold is a compact connected polyhedral cell complex P such that (1) for each point x ∈ P the link Lk(x, P ) is either a PL n-sphere, or is PL-homeomorphic to some M ∈ M; furthermore, if all M ∈ M are oriented, we assume that P is also oriented, and that each homeomorphism Lk(x, P ) → M as above respects the orientations; ∼ (2) for each M ∈ M the set ΛP M := {x ∈ P : Lk(x, P ) = M } is nonempty (if all M ∈ M are oriented, the symbol ∼ = above denotes relation of being PL homeomorphic as oriented manifolds). Recall that hyperbolization, as described e.g. in [DJ], is a procedure that turns an arbitrary compact simplicial complex into a compact nonpositively curved piecewise euclidean polyhedral complex with the same local PL topology. In particular, if we apply this procedure to a simplicial closed M-pseudomanifold P , we get a nonpositively curved piecewise euclidean complex Ph which is also a closed M-pseudomanifold. (If all manifolds in M are oriented, we consider the procedure which respects all requirements concerning orientations, e.g. the procedure described in Subsection (4c) in [DJ], for which each hyperbolized simplex is an oriented manifold, and the associated hyperbolization map is of degree 1.) Observe that the universal cover Peh is then an (M, 0)-pseudomanifold. 11  There is also an analogous procedure, called strict hyperbolization and described in [CD], which turns any simplicial closed M-pseudomanifold P into a piecewise hyperbolic locally CAT (−1) closed M-pseudomanifold Phs . The universal cover Pehs is then an (M, −1)pseudomanifold. Theorem 1 of the introduction is an easy consequence of the following. 5.1 Theorem. Let M = {M1 , . . . , Mk } be a finite family of closed connected PL manifolds of the same dimension n, either all oriented, or at least one of which is non-orientable. Suppose that for some positive integers m1 , . . . , mk the disjoint union ⊔ki=1 mi Mi bounds a compact (n + 1)-dimensional PL manifold W (in oriented sense, if all manifolds from M are oriented). Then there is a hyperbolic group G such that its Gromov boundary ∂G is homeomorphic to the tree of manifolds X (M).  Proof: Consider any PL triangulation of any manifold W as in the assumptions. For each boundary component M of W consider the simplicial cone over M and glue it to W via the identity of M . This gives a simplicial closed M-pseudomanifold which we denote by P . Consider its strict hyperbolization Phs , and its universal cover Pehs , which is a (M, −1)pseudomanifold. The group G = π1 (Phs ) is then a word hyperbolic group, and its Gromov boundary ∂G coincides with the visual boundary ∂ Pehs . Since by Main Theorem the latter boundary is homeomorphic to the tree of manifolds X (M), this completes the proof.  Proof of Theorem 1: Part (1) follows from Theorem 5.1 by taking M = {M }, while part (2) follows by taking M = {N } and W = N × [0, 1].  5.2 Remark. Note that Theorem 1 provides new examples of Gromov boundaries already in dimension 3. More precisely, since each closed connected PL 3-manifold M bounds a compact PL 4-manifold, each tree of 3-manifolds M , X (M ), is homeomorphic to the Gromov boundary of some hyperbolic group. On the other hand, in the case of orientable 3-manifolds the arguments of [PS] (after appropriate correction of the main result of [F] used in [PS]) justify this statement only for manifolds of form M #M , where M and M are the oppositely oriented copies of any orientable 3-manifold. 5.3 Question. Theorem 5.1 leaves open the following question: is there a hyperbolic group G whose Gromov boundary ∂G is homeomorphic to the tree of complex projective planes X (CP 2 )? Recall that neither CP 2 nor any positive number of its copies bounds a compact oriented 5-manifold, so G cannot be obtained by referring to Theorem 5.1. On the other hand, this theorem easily implies that the space X ({CP 2 , CP 2 }) is homeomorphic to the Gromov boundary of some hyperbolic group. Since one can use properties of Čech cohomology rings to show that the spaces X (CP 2 ) and X ({CP 2 , CP 2 }) are not homeomorphic, this also does not help to answer the question. My guess is that the answer is negative. Of course, a similar question can be asked for other than CP 2 manifolds which represent the elements of inﬁnite order in the corresponding oriented cobordism additive semi-group. Coxeter groups with manifold nerves and the proof of Theorem 2. Our main reference concerning Coxeter groups is the book [Da] by Mike Davis. We start with explaining the terms appearing in the statement of Theorem 2, following the terminology and notation from [Da]. 12  Given a ﬁnite set S, a Coxeter matrix on S is a matrix m = (mst )s,t∈S such that (1) for each s ∈ S we have mss = 1; (2) for all s, t ∈ S, s 6= t, we have that mst is an integer ≥ 2 or ∞, and mst = mts . Associated to a Coxeter matrix m on S, there is a group W given by the presentation hS | {(st)mst : s, t ∈ S}i, where the symbol (ab)∞ denotes absence of any relation of the form (ab)k in the set of relations. W is called the Coxeter group associated to m, and it is known that the set S canonically injects in W . The pair (W, S) is called the Coxeter system associated to m. For any subset T ⊂ S the special subgroup WT < W is the subgroup generated by T . It is known that (WT , T ) can be canonically identiﬁed with the Coxeter system associated to the restricted matrix mT , i.e. the matrix (mst )s,t∈T . The nerve of a Coxeter system (W, S) is the simplicial complex L = L(W, S) whose vertex set coincides with S, and such that T ⊂ S spans a simplex of L iﬀ the special subgroup WT is ﬁnite. As it is described in Chapter 7 of [Da], to any Coxeter system (W, S) there is associated a polyhedral cell complex Σ = Σ(W, S), called the Davis-Moussong complex of (W, S), which satisﬁes the following properties: (Σ1) each vertex link of Σ is a simplicial complex isomorphic with the nerve L; (Σ2) Σ carries a natural piecewise euclidean metric with respect to which it is a CAT (0) space, see Theorem 12.3.3 on p. 235 in [Da]; (Σ3) the group W acts on Σ by isometries, properly discontinuously and cocompactly, so that the generators from S correspond to certain geometric reﬂections in Σ, and so that the action is simply transitive on the vertex set of Σ. Proof of Theorem 2: Suppose that the nerve L(W, S) is a PL triangulation of a closed connected manifold M . If M is orientable, it follows from conditions (Σ1)-(Σ3) above that Σ(W, S) is an (M, 0)-pseudomanifold with M = {M, M}, where M and M are the two oppositely oriented copies of M . Similarly, if M is non-orientable, Σ(W, S) is an (M, 0)pseudomanifold with M = {M }. Thus, applying Main Theorem to the Davis-Moussong complex Σ(W, S), we immediately get Theorem 2. 5.4 Remark. It is known that, when a Coxeter group W is word hyperbolic, its Gromov boundary ∂W coincides with the visual boundary ∂Σ(W, S) (see e.g. Remark I.8.5 on p. 527 in [Da]). In such a case, if the nerve L(W, S) is a PL triangulation of a closed connected manifold M , the Gromov boundary ∂W is homeomorphic to the space X (M) as in the statement of Theorem 2. 6. Given a ﬁnite collection M of closed connected manifolds of the same dimension n, a CAT (0) complete geodesic metric space (X, d) is a Riemannian (M, 0)pseudomanifold with log-injective singularities if the following conditions are satisﬁed: (1) there is a subset Λ ⊂ X, called the singular set of X, which is discrete and the complement X \ Λ is a smooth manifold; (2) the set Λ is partitioned into subsets ΛM : M ∈ M such that for each M ∈ M and any p ∈ ΛM the space of directions Σp (X) is homeomorphic to M ; (3) each of the subsets ΛM is a net in X; (4) the regular part X \ Λ is equipped with a Riemannian metric g of nonpositive sectional curvature, such that d restricted to X \ Λ coincides with the path metric induced by g, and the completion of (X \ Λ, d) coincides with (X, d); (5) each p ∈ Λ has a neighborhood U such that the logarithmic map logp : U \ {p} → Σp (X) × R+ (which to each x ∈ U \ {p} associates the pair (a, r) such that a ∈ Σp (X) is the direction of the geodesic [p, x] and r = d(p, x)) is injective; (6) the space Σp (X) at any singular point p has the property that every ball of radius r ∈ (0, π) in it (with respect to the angle metric) is a collared n-disk in Σp (X). Theorem 6.2. Let X be a Riemannian (M, 0)-pseudomanifold with log-injective singularities. Then the visual boundary ∂X is homeomorphic to the tree of manifolds X (M). A nonempty compact subset C of a metric n-manifold (M, d) is cellular if C has arbitrarily close open neighbourhoods which are homeomorphic to the open n-disk. More precisely, given any ǫ > 0 there is a neighbourhood of C of the relevant form mentioned above which is contained in the ǫ-neighbourhood Nǫ (C) = {x ∈ M : d(x, C) < ǫ}. A nonempty compact metric space C is cell-like if it can be embedded into the Hilbert cube Q so that for any neighbourhood U of C in Q the space C is null-homotopic in U . It is known (see e.g. [Ed], remark on p. 114) that a ﬁnite dimensional compact metric space is cell-like if it can be embedded as a cellular subset in some manifold. Obviously, each cellular subset of a manifold is cell-like, but the converse is not true (i.e. there are embeddings of cell-like spaces in manifolds with non-cellular images). A map between metric spaces is cell-like if it is a proper surjection and each point preimage is cell-like. The following result is due to Siebenmann [Si] for n ≥ 5, Quinn [Qu] for n = 4, Armentrout [Ar] for n = 3 and R. L. Moore [Mo] for n ≤ 2. The case of manifolds with 16  nonempty boundary is carefully addressed in [Si], and the cases involving dimension 3 hold because the Poincare conjecture is true. Approximation Theorem. Each cell-like map between manifolds with boundary is a near-homeomorphism, i.e. it can be approximated by homeomorphisms. More precisely, if f : M → N is cell-like and d is any metric in N (compatible with the topology) then for any continuous positive function δ : M → R+ there is a homeomorphism h : M → N such that for all x ∈ M we have d(f (x), h(x)) ≤ δ(x). Let Y be the quotient space obtained from X by shrinking each set f −1 (b) : b ∈ B (viewed as a subset of X) to a point. Since Y is easily seen to be a Hausdorﬀ space, the quotient map F : X → Y is closed, and hence also perfect. Since metrizability is preserved by perfect maps (see e.g. [En], Theorem 4.4.15), Y is metrisable. By deﬁnition of Y , F is a cell-like map.
