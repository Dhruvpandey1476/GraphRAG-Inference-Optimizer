On the Connection Between Learning Two-Layers Neural Networks and Tensor Decomposition  arXiv:1802.07301v1 [cs.LG] 20 Feb 2018  Marco Mondelli* and  Andrea Montanari†  February 22, 2018  Abstract We establish connections between the problem of learning a two-layers neural network with good generalization error and tensor decomposition. We consider a model with input x ∈ Rd , r hidden units with Pr weights {wi }1≤i≤r and output y ∈ R, i.e., y = i=1 σ(hx, wi i), where h·, ·i denotes the scalar product and σ the activation function. First, we show that, if we cannot learn the weights {wi }1≤i≤r accurately, then the neural network does not generalize well. More specifically, the generalization error is close to that of a trivial predictor with access only to the norm of the input. This result holds for any activation function, and it requires that the weights are roughly isotropic and the input distribution is Gaussian, which is a typical assumption in the theoretical literature. Then, we show that the problem of learning the weights {wi }1≤i≤r is at least as hard as the problem of tensor decomposition. This result holds for any input distribution and assumes that the activation function is a polynomial whose degree is related to the order of the tensor to be decomposed. By putting everything together, we prove that learning a two-layers neural network that generalizes well is at least as hard as tensor decomposition. It has been observed that neural network models with more parameters than training samples often generalize well, even if the problem is highly underdetermined. This means that the learning algorithm does not estimate the weights accurately and yet is able to yield a good generalization error. This paper shows that such a phenomenon cannot occur when the input distribution is Gaussian and the weights are roughly isotropic. We also provide numerical evidence supporting our theoretical findings.  1 Introduction Because of the practical success of neural networks, there has been a growing interest in the theoretical understanding of their performance and fundamental limits. Recently, several works have analyzed the optimization landscape of the loss function, characterizing the location, number, and type of stationary points under a variety of assumptions [Kaw16, HM16, SC16, SJL17, SS16, FB16, GLM17]. By using the notion of uniform stability introduced in [BE02], in [HRS16] the authors prove an upper bound on the generalization error in terms of the number of steps of stochastic gradient descent. It has also been observed that the minimization of the training error even in models with more parameters than data often yields a small generalization error: the optimization algorithm, among the many global minima, is biased towards a particular one that generalizes well [SHS17]. This fact suggests * Department †  of Electrical Engineering, Stanford University Department of Electrical Engineering and Department of Statistics, Stanford University  1  that, in order to have good generalization performance, it is not crucial to learn accurately the weights of the neural network. This paper shows that this phenomenon cannot be understood within a setting that is popular in the theoretical literature: Gaussian features and isotropic weight vectors in the generative model (at least for the case of two-layers neural networks). Recovery guarantees have been proved for deep neural networks with sparse weights in the generative model [ABGM14], for convolutional neural networks [BG17] and for feedfoward neural networks with one or multiple hidden layers [SA15, JSA15, ZLJ16, Tia17, ZSJ+ 17]. The existing algorithms employ kernel methods [ZLJ16] and gradient descent [Tia17, BG17], which is typically combined with tensor methods for initialization [SA15, JSA15, ZSJ+ 17]. The connection between tensors and neural networks is also investigated in [GLM17], where it is shown that the population risk is the average of an infinite number of loss functions for tensor decomposition. Here, we investigate the computational hardness of learning and generalizing a two-layers neural network in the realizable setting and we relate it to the problem of tensor decomposition. More formally, we consider the following model: r X σ(hx, wi i), (1) y(x) = i=1  where x ∈ Rd is the input, y ∈ R is the output, {wi }1≤i≤r are the unknown weights, σ is the known activation function, and h·, ·i denotes the scalar product. The main contributions of this paper are summarized below. 1. We show that, if we cannot learn the weights {wi }1≤i≤r accurately, then the generalization error of the predictor ŷ(x) is close to that of a trivial predictor with access only to the norm of the input. This result holds for any activation function σ, and it relies crucially on the fact that the input distribution is Gaussian and the weights are roughly isotropic. 2. We present reductions from the problem of tensor decomposition to the problem of estimating the weights {wi }1≤i≤r . We show that, if there is no polynomial algorithm that solves tensor decomposition, then there is no polynomial algorithm that learns the weights of a two-layers neural network. These results rely crucially on the fact that the activation function is a polynomial whose degree is related to the order of the tensor to be decomposed and hold for any input distribution. 3. By combining the previous two results, we give a reduction from the problem of tensor decomposition to the problem of finding a predictor ŷ(x) with small generalization error. We show that, if there is no polynomial algorithm that solves tensor decomposition, then there is no polynomial algorithm that provides a predictor ŷ(x) with a better generalization than that of a trivial predictor. We also present numerical experiments supporting our theoretical findings. In particular, we show that, as the number of weights becomes significantly larger than the dimension, learning the weights becomes harder and, consequently, the generalization error gets closer to that of a trivial predictor. As we previously pointed out, recent works giving recovery guarantees on the problem of learning the weights of two-layers 2  neural networks employ tensor methods as an initialization step. Hence, our reductions from tensor decomposition to learning a two-layers neural network can be regarded as a converse to those results. In a nutshell, our results suggest that a popular model for theoretical research (Gaussian feature vectors x and true labels produced according to (1)) is not consistent with empirical observations on the performance of neural networks on real data. Namely, (i) learning in this model requires accurate weight estimation which does not appear to occur in practice; and (ii) the latter requires solving a tensor decomposition problem, which is also not common in practice. We believe that different probabilistic models might provide better insight. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. After describing more in detail some related works, in Section 2 we present the system model and some existing results about tensor decomposition. These results will be useful as our reductions assume that tensor decomposition is hard. In Section 3, we state and discuss our main contributions. The proofs of the generalization bound and of the reductions to tensor decomposition are contained in Section 4 and 5, respectively. In Section 6, we present the numerical experiments.  1.1 Related Works Classical neural networks theory shows that learning is information-theoretically possible with linear sample complexity and provide risk bounds with unbounded computational complexity [Bar94, Bar98]. However, most of the worst case results are negative [BR89, BBD99, Kuh00, Ším02]. In particular, training even a neural network with a single neuron is NP-hard [Ším02]. As mentioned earlier, the optimization landscape for the problem of learning a neural network has been studied in several works. In particular, [CHM+ 15] and [Kaw16] make an independence assumption1 that does not hold in practice. Residual networks are studied in [HM16], piecewise linear activation functions in [SC16], and quadratic activation functions in [SJL17]. The quality of the initialization is considered in [SS16], and the topology and geometry of the loss surface and level sets are analyzed in [FB16]. A loss function that provides a good and provable optimization landscape is designed in [GLM17]. The interplay between generalization and training error is considered in [ZBH+ 16], which shows empirically that practical neural networks are significantly overparametrized. A few recent papers [ABGM14, BG17, SA15, JSA15, ZLJ16, Tia17, ZSJ+ 17] provide recovery guarantees for several neural network models. In [ABGM14], the weights are assumed to be sparse and randomly distributed in [−1, 1], and the proposed algorithm learns almost all the models in this class with polynomial sample complexity and computational complexity. In [BG17], the authors consider a two-layers neural network with convolutional structure, no overlap2 , and ReLU activation function. It is shown that learning is NP-complete in the worst case, but gradient descent converges to the global optimum in polynomial time when the input distribution is Gaussian. However, the assumption of no overlap is typically not fulfilled in practice. A similar positive result, i.e., convergence to the global optimum of gradient descent with polynomial complexity and Gaussian input, is proved in [Tia17]. In this work, the author considers a two-layers neural network model of the form (1), where σ is a ReLU activation function and the weights {wi }1≤i≤r are orthogonal (which immediately implies that r ≤ d). However, [Tia17] requires a good initialization and does not discuss initialization methods. In [SA15], the subspace spanned by the weight matrix is provably recovered with a tensor algorithm, and the weights can also be recovered under an additional sparsity assumption. Furthermore, the works [BG17, Tia17, SA15] consider only the population risk and do not give bounds on the sample complexity. In [JSA15], it is presented a tensor algorithm that learns a two-layers 1 In most neural network models, the output is related to each of the input nodes via several different paths, which leads to dependencies, while [CHM+ 15] and [Kaw16] consider a fully decoupled model. 2 The filter of the convolutional neural network is applied to non-overlapping parts of the input vector.  3  neural network with sample complexity of order d3 · poly(r)/ε2 , where ε is the precision. In [ZSJ+ 17], a tensor initialization algorithm is combined with gradient descent to obtain a procedure with sample complexity of order d · poly(r) · log(1/ε) and computational complexity n · d · poly(r) · log(1/ε), where n is the number of samples and it is assumed that r ≤ d. As concerns hardness results, in [Sha16] it is proved that it is not enough to have either a “nice” input distribution or a “nice” target functions to guarantee computationally tractable learning. On the one hand, when the activation function is a ReLU, no algorithm invariant to linear transformations can successfully learn the neural network with respect to all input distributions in polynomial time. On the other hand, some specific periodic functions are difficult to learn with gradient-based method even when the input distribution belongs to a broad class of smooth distributions, including Gaussians and mixtures of Gaussians.  2 Preliminaries 2.1 Notation and System Model Let [n] be a shorthand for {1, . . . , n}. Similarly to the problem of learning a neural network, many problems involving tensors (e.g., the computation of the rank or the spectral norm) are NP-hard in the worst case [Hås90, HL13]. We consider a two-layers neural network with input dimension d = 50 and r hidden nodes, with r ∈ {50, 350, 2500}. The activation function σ is equal to tanh (5x/2). Note that σ̂1 ≈ 0.376 and σ̂2 = 0. The weights {wi }1≤i≤r are obtained by concatenating r/d random unitary matrices of size d × d that are independent and identically distributed according to the Haar measure. In particular, each of these matrices is obtained from the SVD of a matrix whose entries that are ∼i.i.d. N(0, 1).
