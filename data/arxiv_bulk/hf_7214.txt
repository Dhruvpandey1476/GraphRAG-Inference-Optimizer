On the one hand, their geometry is sufficiently rich to ensure that large classes of groups admit interesting actions on them. Right-angled Artin groups, hyperbolic or right-angled Coxeter groups, hyperbolic 3-manifold groups, random groups at sufficiently low density all act geometrically on CAT(0) cube complexes [NR03, BW12, OW11], to name just a few examples. Moreover, every finitely generated group with a codimension one subgroup admits an action on a CAT(0) cube complex with unbounded orbits [Sag95, Ger97, NR98]. On the other hand, the geometry of finite dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes is much better understood than general CAT(0) geometry, even with no local compactness assumption. For instance, groups acting properly on 1  2  ELIA FIORAVANTI  finite dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes are known to have finite asymptotic dimension [Wri12], to satisfy the Von Neumann-Day dichotomy and, if torsion-free, even the Tits alternative [SW05, CS11]. It is not known whether the same are true for general CAT(0) groups. Three features are particularly relevant in the study of CAT(0) cube complexes. First of all, they are endowed with a metric of non-positive curvature. Secondly, the 1-skeleton becomes a median graph when endowed with its intrinsic path-metric; this means that, for any three vertices, there exists a unique vertex that lies between any two of them. This property is closely related to the existence of hyperplanes and allows for a combinatorial approach that is not available in general CAT(0) spaces. Finally, cube complexes are essentially discrete objects, in that their geometry is fully encoded by the 0skeleton. In particular, the automorphism group of a cube complex is totally disconnected. It is natural to wonder how much in the theory of CAT(0) cube complexes can be extended to those spaces that share the second feature: median spaces. These provide a simultaneous generalisation of CAT(0) cube complexes and real trees; for an introduction, see e.g. [Nic08, CDH10, Bow16] and references therein. The class of median spaces is closed under ultralimits and also includes all L1 spaces, so certain pathologies are bound to arise in this context. The notion of rank of a median space was introduced in [Bow13]; for CAT(0) cube complexes it coincides with the usual concept of dimension. It was recently shown that connected, finite rank median spaces are also endowed with a canonical CAT(0) metric [Bow16]. Thus it seems that, in finite rank, the only true difference between CAT(0) cube complexes and general median spaces lies in the discreteness of the former. Most of the results of the present paper support this analogy. We will restrict our attention to finite rank median spaces; this is essential when considering the Tits alternative, as every amenable group admits a proper action on an infinite rank median space [CMV04, CDH10]. Nontrivial examples of finite rank median spaces arise, for instance, from asymptotic cones of coarse median spaces [Bow13, Zei16]. The Cayley graphs of many interesting groups are coarse median: hyperbolic groups, cubulated groups [HS16], fundamental groups of closed irreducible 3-manifolds not modelled on Nil or Sol, mapping class groups [BM11, BDS11b, BDS11a] and, more generally, all groups that are HHS [Bow15a, BHS17, BHS15]. Our main result is the following version of the Tits alternative: Theorem A. Let X be a complete, finite rank median space with an isometric action Γ y X. Suppose that Γ has no nonabelian free subgroups. • If the action is free, Γ is virtually finite-by-abelian. If moreover X is connected or Γ is finitely generated, then Γ is virtually abelian. • If the action is (metrically) proper, Γ is virtually (locally finite)-byabelian.  THE TITS ALTERNATIVE FOR FINITE RANK MEDIAN SPACES  3  • If all point stabilisers are amenable, Γ is amenable. We remark that every countable, locally finite group admits a proper action on a simplicial tree, see Example II.7.11 in [BH99]. A discrete group Γ has the Haagerup property if and only if it admits a proper action on a median space [CMV04, CDH10]. However, Theorem A shows that Γ needs not have a proper (or free) action on a finite rank median space; for instance, it suffices to consider any torsion-free amenable group that is not virtually abelian. In fact, there even exist groups Γ with the Haagerup property, such that every action of Γ on a complete, finite rank median space has a global fixed point; a simple example is provided by irreducible lattices in SL2 R × SL2 R [Fio17b]. Our proof of Theorem A follows the same broad outline as the corresponding result for CAT(0) cube complexes, as it appears in [CS11, CFI16]. Given an action Γ y X, either Γ has a finite orbit within a suitable compactification of X, or the space X exhibits a certain tree-like behaviour. In the former case, one obtains a “big” abelian quotient of Γ; in the latter, one can construct many nonabelian free subgroups with a ping-pong argument. We remark that the first proof of the Tits alternative for CAT(0) cube complexes was due to M. Sageev and D. T. Wise [SW05] and follows a completely different strategy. It relies on the Algebraic Torus Theorem [DS00] and a key fact is that, when a group Γ acts nontrivially on a CAT(0) cube complex, some hyperplane stabiliser is a codimension one subgroup of Γ. Unfortunately, this approach is bound to fail when dealing with median spaces: there is an analogous notion of “hyperplane” (see Section 2.1), but all hyperplane stabilisers could be trivial, even if the group Γ is one-ended. This happens for instance when a surface group acts freely on a real tree [MS91]. Many of the techniques of [CS11] have proven extremely useful in the study of CAT(0) cube complexes, for instance in [NS13, Fer15, CFI16, KS16a, KS16b] to name just a few examples. We extend some of this machinery to median spaces, in particular what goes by the name of “flipping”, “skewering” and “strong separation”; see Theorems B and D below. We will exploit these results in [Fio17b] to obtain a superrigidity result analogous to the one in [CFI16]. To state the rest of the results of the present paper, we need to introduce some terminology. In [Fio17a] we defined a compactification X of a complete, finite rank median space X. We refer to it as the Roller compactification of X; for CAT(0) cube complexes, it consists precisely of the union of X and its Roller boundary ∂X, in the usual sense (see [BCG+ 09, NS13] for a definition). Roller compactifications of general median spaces strongly resemble Roller compactifications of cube complexes. For instance, the space X has a natural structure of median algebra and ∂X is partitioned as a union of median spaces (“components” in our terminology), whose rank is strictly lower than  4  ELIA FIORAVANTI  that of X. These properties will be essential when extending the machinery of [CS11] to finite rank median spaces. For CAT(0) cube complexes, our approach is slightly different from that of [CS11], in that we work with Roller boundaries rather than visual boundaries. We say that an isometric action Γ y X is Roller elementary if Γ has at least one finite orbit in X. An action Γ y X is Roller minimal if Γ leaves invariant no proper, closed, convex subset of X and X is not a single point; for the notion of convexity in the median algebra X, see Section 2.1. In CAT(0) cube complexes, Roller minimal actions are precisely essential actions (in the terminology of [CS11]) with no fixed point in the visual boundary. Like CAT(0) cube complexes, median spaces are endowed with a canonical collection of halfspaces H . We say that Γ y X is without wall inversions if there exists no g ∈ Γ such that gh = h∗ for some h ∈ H . Here h∗ denotes the complement X \ h of the halfspace h. Actions without wall inversions are a generalisation of actions on 0-skeleta of simplicial trees without edge inversions. We remark that, perhaps counterintuitively, every action on a connected median space is automatically without wall inversions (see Proposition 2.3 below); this includes all actions on real trees. We have the following analogue of the Flipping and Double Skewering Lemmata from [CS11]. Theorem B. Let X be a complete, finite rank median space with a Roller minimal action Γ y X without wall inversions. • For “almost every” halfspace h, there exists g ∈ Γ with h∗ ( gh and d(gh∗ , h∗ ) > 0. • For “almost all” halfspaces h, k with h ⊆ k there exists g ∈ Γ with gk ( h ⊆ k and d(gk, h∗ ) > 0. Positivity of distances is automatic in cube complexes, but not in general median spaces. Theorem B cannot be proved for all halfspaces; counterexamples already appear in real trees, see Section 5. The notion of “almost every” should be understood with respect to a certain measure on H ; see Section 2.1 below for a definition. For every complete, finite rank median space X, we can define a barycentric subdivision X 0 ; we study these in Section 2.3. The space X 0 is again complete and median of the same rank. There is a canonical isometric embedding X ,→ X 0 and every action on X extends to an action without wall inversions on X 0 . Thus, the assumption that Γ y X be without wall inversions in Theorem B is not restrictive. More precisely, we need strongly separated pairs of halfspaces; in the case of CAT(0) cube complexes, these were introduced in [BC12] and used in [CS11] to characterise irreducibility. We prove: Theorem D. Let X be a complete, finite rank median space admitting a Roller minimal action Γ y X without wall inversions. The last step in the proof of Theorem A consists in the study of Roller elementary actions; we employ the same strategy as the appendix to [CFI16]. A finite sequence of points (xk )1≤k≤n is a geodesic if d(x1 , xn ) = d(x1 , x2 ) + ... + d(xn−1 , xn ). The interval I(x, y) between x, y ∈ X is the set of points lying on a geodesic from x to y. We say that X is a median space if, for all x, y, z ∈ X, the intersection I(x, y)∩I(y, z)∩I(z, x) consists of a single point, which we denote by m(x, y, z). In this case, the map m : X 3 → X endows X with a structure of median algebra, see e.g. [CDH10, Bow13, Rol98] for a definition and various results. In a median algebra (M, m), the interval I(x, y) between x, y ∈ M is the set of points z ∈ M with m(x, y, z) = z; this is equivalent to the definition above if M arises from a median space. A subset C ⊆ M is convex if I(x, y) ⊆ C whenever x, y ∈ C. Every collection of pairwise-intersecting convex subsets of a median algebra has the finite intersection property; this is Helly’s Theorem, see e.g. Theorem 2.2 in [Rol98]. A halfspace is a convex subset h ⊆ M whose complement h∗ := M \ h is also convex. We will refer to the unordered pair w := {h, h∗ } as a wall ; we say that h and h∗ are the sides of w. The wall w separates subsets A ⊆ M and B ⊆ M if A ⊆ h and B ⊆ h∗ or vice versa. The wall w is contained in a halfspace k if h ⊆ k or h∗ ⊆ k; we say, with a slight abuse of terminology, that w is contained in k1 ∩ ... ∩ kk if w is contained in ki for each i. The sets of halfspaces and walls of M are denoted H (M ) and W (M ) respectively, or simply H and W . Given subsets A, B ⊆ M , we write H (A|B) for the set of halfspaces with B ⊆ h and A ⊆ h∗ and we set  THE TITS ALTERNATIVE FOR FINITE RANK MEDIAN SPACES  7  σA := H (∅|A); we will not distinguish between x ∈ M and the singleton {x}. If A, B are convex and disjoint, we have H (A|B) 6= ∅, see e.g. Theorem 2.7 in [Rol98]; we will refer to the sets H (x|y), x, y ∈ M , as halfspace-intervals. A pocset is a poset equipped with an order-reversing involution ∗ such that every element a is incomparable with a∗ . Ordering H by inclusion, we obtain a pocset where the involution is given by taking complements. If E ⊆ H , we write E ∗ := {h∗ | h ∈ E}. We say that h, k ∈ H are transverse if any two elements in the set {h, h∗ , k, k∗ } are incomparable; equivalently, the intersections h ∩ k, h ∩ k∗ , h∗ ∩ k, h∗ ∩ k∗ are all nonempty subsets of M . Two walls are transverse if they arise from transverse halfspaces. Maximal subsets of H consisting of pairwise-intersecting halfspaces are termed ultrafilters; a set of pairwise-intersecting halfspaces is an ultrafilter if and only if it contains a side of every wall of M . For every x ∈ M , the subset σx ⊆ H is an ultrafilter. A subset Ω ⊆ H is said to be inseparable if it contains all j ∈ H such that h ⊆ j ⊆ k, for h, k ∈ Ω. The inseparable closure of a subset Ω ⊆ H is the smallest inseparable set containing Ω; it coincides the union of the sets H (k∗ |h) as h, k vary in Ω.
