Approximating the Permanent of a Random Matrix with Vanishing Mean Lior Eldar *, Saeed Mehraban †  arXiv:1711.09457v1 [cs.DS] 26 Nov 2017  November 28, 2017  Abstract Building upon a recent approach pioneered by Barvinok [4, 5, 7, 8] we present a quasi polynomial time algorithm for approximating the permanent of a typical n × n random matrix with unit variance and vanishing mean µ = (ln ln n)−1/6 to within inverse polynomial multiplicative error. This result counters the common intuition that the difficulty of computing the permanent, even approximately, stems merely from our inability to treat matrices with many opposing signs. Our result applies, in particular, to the Gaussian matrix A ∼ N (µ, 1, C)n×n for such µ. On the other hand, the BosonSampling paradigm [2] relies, in part, on a conjecture that approximating the permanent of typical Gaussian matrices with mean µ = 0, or effectively µ = 1/poly(n), is #P -hard. Thus, our range of parameters comes tantalizingly close to the threshold of this conjectured hardness and raises the intriguing question of whether there exists a yet undiscovered phenomenon, that occurs only for inverse-polynomial values of the mean, which prohibits efficient approximation, or whether that conjecture is false. Our approximation algorithm relies upon a technique we develop here called “computational analytic continuation”, that was also suggested by Barvinok [5, 6]. In this method a complex function is approximated along an analytical curve in the complex plane that avoids all poles (or any other singularity) of the function with a significant margin. The algorithm computes many derivatives of the function at an initial point along the curve where derivative computation is relatively easy, and then extends the value of the function, and a subset of its derivatives further along the curve to a point of interest where direct computation of derivatives is much harder. We complement this technique with a random polynomial analysis: we employ an average-case version of Jensen’s formula to analyze the location of the roots of a certain univariate polynomial related to the permanent. Employing these two techniques in conjunction allows us to extend the known range for computing the permanent well beyond what was previously known. We believe that our approach may have several implications to understanding the permanent in the context of computational complexity, anti-concentration statistics, and de-randomization. * eldar.lior@gmail.com †  MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL), mehraban@mit.edu  1  1 1.1  Introduction Complexity of Computing the Permanent  The permanent of an n × n matrix A is the following degree n polynomial in the entries of A: Per(A) :=  n XY  Ai,σ(i) .  (1)  σ∈Sn i=1  Its computation has been the subject of intense research [5, 2, 12, 11, 17, 13] and has been connected to subjects varying from random sampling of bi-partite matchings [12] to establishing a so-called “quantum supremacy” using linear optical experiments [2]. Much of this interest is centered on the computational complexity of computing the permanent. The permanent is known to be #P -hard to compute exactly, and we only know exponential time algorithms for computing the permanent of a general matrix A, the fastest of which is the Ryser formula. Given the apparent difficulty in computing the permanent exactly it is natural to allow the algorithm to err on some small set of inputs. However, it turns out that it is still difficult to compute the permanent even on most inputs. It has been shown [9] that if one can compute the permanent on a fraction 1/poly(n) of all n × n matrices whose entries are chosen from some field Fp then the permanent can be computed on all such matrices over Fp . Specifically for the case of Gaussian a somewhat weaker statement is true: it is #P -hard to compute Per(A) exactly for a Gaussian A w.p. greater than 3/4 + 1/poly(n) [2]. Since allowing an algorithm to err on a small fraction of the inputs doesn’t seem to make permanent computation any easier research has also focused on computing an approximation for the value of permanent: for multiplicative approximation which is the focus of this work approximation schemes are known for several special cases of matrices. Perhaps most prominently is the work of Jerrum, Sinclair and Vigoda [12] who showed a polynomial time algorithm to compute a 1 + 1/poly(n) multiplicative approximation of the permanent for matrices with non-negative entries. More recently [3] have shown how to approximate the permanent of a PSD matrix to simply exponential factor in polynomial time. Still, for general matrices, it seems that efficient approximation of the permanent remains well out of reach and progress is limited by a an “interference barrier” by which for a general matrix the positive and negative entries may generate an intricate interference pattern that is hard to approximate using combinatorial methods. Given the apparent robustness of the complexity of computing the permanent against both error on a fraction of inputs, and against approximation of the value of the permanent itself Aaronson and Arkhipov [2] designed a beautiful paradigm called BosonSampling for demonstrating a so-called quantum supremacy over classical computers. It hinges upon permanent computation remaining #P -hard even when we simultaneously allow error on some inputs, and allow an approximate value for the inputs for which we do handle. The intuitive difficulty of approximating the permanent on such a distribution stems from the fact that a typical Gaussian matrix contains many entries with opposing signs and hence would resist any combinatorial-type approximation algorithm. We discuss this further in Section 1.5.1.  1.2  The Complex Function Perspective and Its Limitations  Recent works of Barvinok [4, 5, 7, 8] have outlined a new approach to computing the permanent, and in fact a large class of high-degree polynomials in matrices such as the partition function [15, 14]. His approach, stated here for the permanent of matrix A, is the following: Instead of trying to compute Per(A) directly, we compute an additive approximation of ln(Per(A)) and then exponentiate the result. The value of ln(Per(A)) is then computed as a Taylor series approximation of the complex log of the univariate polynomial g(z) = Per(J·(1−z)+z·A) around  2  the point z = 0, and evaluated at point z = 1. The crucial point is that one can compute the lowest m derivatives of f at point z = 0 relatively efficiently, i.e., in time nO(m) since they correspond essentially to a linear combination of the permanents of the lowest m × m minors of A. The additive approximation error of the Taylor series expansion decays exponentially fast in m 2 thereby leading potentially to a 2O(ln (n)) algorithm for a multiplicative error of 1 + 1/poly(n). That said, there is a significant caveat that must be carefully handled: the Taylor approximation of ln(g(z)) about a point z = x0 is valid only in a disk around x0 that contains no poles of f (z) (the roots of g(z)). Therefore, this approach inherently requires knowledge about the location of the roots of the univariate polynomial used for interpolating the value of the permanent from an easy-to-compute matrix to the target matrix A. Nevertheless, using these insights, Barvinok was able to show that for matrices A such that maxi,j |Ai,j − 1| ≤ c0 for the constant c0 = 0.5 one can in fact compute a 1 + 1/poly approximation of the permanent in quasi-polynomial time. Thus, this approach is the first to break the “sign barrier” for approximating the permanent, albeit for a moderate interference pattern: the entries of the matrices that the algorithm handles are still highly correlated in a specific direction. Still, this approach opens up a wide range of possibilities for computing these otherwise intractable polynomials: instead of thinking about them as combinatorial objects, one can examine this new approach and ask a completely different set of questions: what can be said about the location of the poles of f (z)? can one detour around these poles in order to reach “interesting” points z where the value of ef (z) is non-trivial? An even more general set of questions can then be phrased for a random ensemble of matrices A: what is the “typical” distribution of the roots of g(z) and can they be detoured “on average”, in an efficient way? Answering such questions analytically, and subsequently efficiently as an algorithm is the focus of this work.  1.3  Main Results  We now state the main results of this paper. This significantly increases the regime of matrices for which we can find a reasonably efficient algorithm for approximating the permanent, and places in serious doubt the intuition that the permanent is hard because of the “opposing sign problem”. We now further dwell on its implications on other aspects of the permanent:  1.5.1  Implications to Boson Sampling  In [2] the authors consider the following computational problem Definition 4 (GPEµ× ). given A ∼ N n×n (µ, 1, C), ε, δ, output a number Q such that with probability at least 1 − δ, Q − Per(A) ≤ ε|Per(A)| in poly(n, 1/ε, 1/δ). This raises the following intriguing question: it seems that the hardness of the permanent of Gaussian matrices (or general matrices for that matter) is not due to the common intuition that the different signs of the entries prohibits combinatorial treatment of the matrix, as a graph, in the spirit of [12]. Approximating gA (z) for typical A at |z| = 1/µ implies an approximation of the permanent for a random matrix with mean µ. However, one can see from the upper-bound on the interpolation error above that in order to achieve sufficiently small error the number of derivatives we initially compute must scale exponentially in the ratio σ/∆, namely the ratio of the interpolation length σ, and the step size ∆. Since ∆ ∼ 1/Nσ where Nσ = Ω(σ 2 ) is the number of roots in the disk of radius σ, then ∆ ∼ 1/σ 2 which implies that the number of required derivatives is exponential in poly(σ). Thus to improve on our technique new ideas would be required which would make a more economic use of the derivatives computed, and not “discard” at each step i a fraction 1 − 1/ ln(si ) of the si derivatives computed at that step. Another approach would be to tighten the bound on the number of roots inside the disk of a given radius for the polynomial gA (z) or some other related polynomial.  1.5.3  Anti-Concentration of the Permanent  We point out that our algorithm, and also that of [5, 8], are not merely a sequence of computational steps, but actually show that the permanent of typical Gaussian (or more generally, random) matrices A ∼ N (µ, 1, C)n×n are well approximated by a low-degree polynomial in the entries of A ∼ N (0, 1, C)n×n - in fact a polynomial of degree polylog(n). Such a statement strongly indicates that for this range the permanent of Gaussian matrices is anti-concentrated, since it is well-known by a theorem of Carbery-Wright [10] that any polynomial of low-degree in standard i.i.d. Gaussian variables is anti-concentrated. However, we have been unable to use this theorem directly to derive a formal anti - concentration statement. We note that the anti-concentration of the Gaussian permanent is also a necessary conjecture of the BosonSampling paradigm in [2, 1] along with the conjectured #P -hardness of this problem. Hence, intriguingly it seems that the conjectures on the computability, and statistics (i.e. anti-concentration) of the permanent of Gaussian are closely related: our results indicate that for mean values 1/polylog(n) the anti-concentration conjecture holds, but ironically this is because the second conjecture does not hold for this regime - i.e. the permanent is relatively easy to compute via a polynomial of low degree.  1.5.4  De-randomization  For the special case of non-negative matrices the celebrated algorithm of Jerrum, Sinclair and Vigoda [12] computes a 1 + 1/poly approximation of the permanent. Yet it seems that derandomizing this algorithm poses a significant challenge: as far as we know, the best deterministic algorithm for this problem is due to Linial, Samorodnitsky and Wigderson [13] that computes only an exponential multiplicative approximation in polynomial time, using matrix scaling. Using a slight variation of our algorithm we believe one can easily show the following: Corollary 6. Let A be a uniformly random n × n matrix where each entry is either 1/poly(ln ln(n)) w.p. 1/2 or 1 − 1/poly(ln ln(n)) w.p. 1/2. There exists a quasi-poly-time deterministic algorithm that computes Per(A) to multiplicative error 1 + 1/poly, for a fraction 1 − o(1) of matrices. Thus our approach results in a deterministic algorithm for “most” matrices which are “almost” {0, 1} matrices. Can our approach be extended further in this respect to de-randomize the algorithm for {0, 1} matrices? Does it imply de-randomization for other classes of nonnegative matrices?  7  2  Preliminaries  2.1  Notation  C denotes the complex numbers, R denotes the real numbers.
