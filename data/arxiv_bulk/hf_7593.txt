arXiv:1708.00846v2 [math.AC] 7 Sep 2017  FLAT MORPHISMS OF FINITE PRESENTATION ARE VERY FLAT LEONID POSITSELSKI AND ALEXANDER SLÁVIK Abstract. Let R be a commutative ring and S be a finitely presented commutative R-algebra. In this paper we show that if S is a flat R-module, then S is a very flat R-module. This proves a conjecture formulated in the February 2014 version of the preprint [20]. We also show that the (finite) very flatness property of a flat module satisfies descent with respect to commutative ring homomorphisms of finite presentation inducing surjective maps of the spectra. Introduction 0.1. The abelian category of modules over a ring has enough projective objects, which can be used in order to construct left derived functors. Right derived functors can be constructed using injective modules. The abelian category of quasi-coherent sheaves on a scheme also has enough injective objects but, generally speaking, no projectives. On a quasi-projective scheme, there are enough invertible sheaves; and some other schemes may have enough locally free sheaves, of finite or infinite rank. Locally free sheaves of possibly infinite rank, or what is the same over a locally Noetherian scheme [1, Corollary 4.5], locally projective sheaves, actually form a quite remarkable class. In particular, the projectivity of a module over a commutative ring 1  is a local property, satisfying descent with respect to Zariski coverings, and in fact, even with respect to faithfully flat ring morphisms [25, 15]. But what if there are not enough locally projective sheaves? A Noetherian scheme is said to have the resolution property if it has enough locally free sheaves of finite rank [28]. It seems to be an open question whether there exist algebraic varieties that do not have the resolution property. On the other hand, on any quasi-compact semi-separated scheme there are enough flat quasi-coherent sheaves [11, Section 2.4], [4, Lemma A.1]. But arbitrary flat modules over a commutative ring are much more complicated, from the homological point of view, than the projective modules. In particular, flat modules over a ring that is not necessarily Noetherian of finite Krull dimension form an exact category of infinite homological dimension. 0.2. In fact, the derived category of the exact category of flat modules over an associative ring was studied and shown to be equivalent to the homotopy category of projective modules [13]. Extending this approach to schemes, one can define the mock homotopy category of (nonexistent) projective quasi-coherent sheaves on a quasi-compact semi-separated scheme as the derived category of the exact category of flat quasi-coherent sheaves [12]. One of the potential shortcomings of this approach is that it is not directly applicable to matrix factorizations. Matrix factorizations are a kind of curved differential modules, so the conventional construction of the derived category does not make sense for them. Instead, one has to use derived categories of the second kind, such as the coderived and the absolute derived categories [14, 4]. However, for an exact category of infinite homological dimension, the coderived category may differ from the derived category [17, Section 2.1], [18, Examples 3.3]. One of the approaches suggested in [4, Remark 2.6] is to use the very flat quasicoherent sheaves (which were previously defined in [20, Section 4.1]) in lieu of the locally free or flat ones. The aim of this paper is to show just how well this approach turns out to work. 0.3. For every quasi-compact semi-separated scheme X, there is a full subcategory of very flat quasi-coherent sheaves X–qcohvfl ⊂ X–qcoh in the abelian category X–qcoh of quasi-coherent sheaves on X having the following properties: (VF1) All very flat quasi-coherent sheaves are flat, that is X–qcohvfl ⊂ X–qcohfl , where X–qcohfl ⊂ X–qcoh denotes the full subcategory of flat quasi-coherent sheaves on X. (VF2) All locally projective quasi-coherent sheaves are very flat, that is X–qcohlp ⊂ X–qcohvfl , where X–qcohlp ⊂ X–qcoh denotes the full subcategory of locally projective quasi-coherent sheaves on X. (VF3) The tensor product of any two very flat quasi-coherent sheaves is very flat, that is X–qcohvfl ⊗OX X–qcohvfl ⊂ X–qcohvfl .  2  (VF4) The full subcategory X–qcohvfl ⊂ X–qcoh is closed under extensions, infinite direct sums, direct summands, and the passages to the kernels of surjective morphisms of quasi-coherent sheaves. (VF5) Every quasi-coherent sheaf on X is the quotient sheaf of a very flat quasicoherent sheaf. The properties (VF4–VF5) can be summarized by saying that X–qcohvfl is a resolving subcategory in X–qcoh (closedness under direct sums is an extra property not covered by the definition of a resolving subcategory). In particular, it follows that the full subcategory X–qcohvfl inherits an exact category structure from the abelian category X–qcoh. (VF6) The exact category X–qcohvfl has finite homological dimension, not exceeding the number of affine open subschemes in an affine open covering of a quasicompact semi-separated scheme X. (VF7) The embedding of exact categories X–qcohvfl −→ X–qcohfl induces an equivalence of their derived categories, D(X–qcohvfl ) ≃ D(X–qcohfl ). The properties (VF1–VF5) hold for the class of all flat quasi-coherent sheaves in place of the class of very flat quasi-coherent sheaves just as well, but the property (VF6) does not. In particular, it means that any very flat module ( = very flat quasi-coherent sheaf over an affine scheme) has projective dimension not exceeding 1. But even flat modules of projective dimension 1 can be complicated. The following property shows the difference between a very flat module and an arbitrary module of flat dimension 1. Given a scheme X and a scheme point x ∈ X, let us view x as a scheme (namely, the spectrum of the residue field kx (X) of the point x ∈ X) and denote by ιx : x −→ X the natural morphism. For any quasi-coherent sheaf F on X, the P-support PSuppX F ⊂ X of F is the set of all points x ∈ X such that the quasi-coherent sheaf (or, which is the same in this case, the kx (X)-vector space) ι∗x F does not vanish. (VF8) For any very flat quasi-coherent sheaf F on X, the subset PSuppX F ⊂ X is open in the Zariski topology of X. Notice that the property (VF8) certainly does not hold for arbitrary flat modules or quasi-coherent sheaves, as the example of the flat Z-module Q (whose P-support consists of the single generic point of Spec Z) already demonstrates. On the other hand, the properties (VF1–VF4) and (VF8) hold for the class of all locally projective quasi-coherent sheaves in place of the class of all very flat ones. When the analogue of (VF5) holds for locally projective quasi-coherent sheaves, it follows that the analogues of (VF6–VF7) also do. 0.4. Further important properties of the class of very flat quasi-coherent sheaves are related to changing schemes. Let S f : Y −→ X be a morphism of quasi-compact semi-separated schemes, and let X = α Uα be an open covering of the scheme X. (VF9) The inverse image functor f ∗ takes very flat quasi-coherent sheaves on X to very flat quasi-coherent sheaves on Y , that is f ∗ (X–qcohvfl ) ⊂ Y –qcohvfl . 3  (VF10) The very flatness property of a quasi-coherent sheaf on X satisfies Zariski descent, that is a quasi-coherent sheaf F on X is very flat if and only the quasi-coherent sheaf F |Uα is very flat on Uα for every α. Of course, the classes of flat quasi-coherent sheaves and locally projective quasicoherent sheaves also satisfy their similar versions of (VF9–VF10). All of the above having been mentioned, one remaining major property one would like to have for the class of very flat quasi-coherent sheaves is the preservation by direct images with respect to good enough affine morphisms. For example, the class of flat quasi-coherent sheaves is preserved by direct images with respect to all flat affine morphisms f : Y −→ X, where an affine morphism f is said to be flat if the OX (U)-module OY (f −1 (U)) is flat for every affine open subscheme U ⊂ X. Equivalently, a morphism f is flat if the local ring Oy (Y ) is a flat module over the local ring Ox (X) for every pair of points x ∈ X and y ∈ Y such that f (y) = x. What can one say about the preservation of very flatness by the direct images? What are the very flat affine morphisms of schemes? Notice first of all that, according to (VF10), the question reduces to affine schemes, so it suffices to discuss the very flat morphisms of commutative rings. What are these? Before we begin to answer this question, let us define, at long last, the very flat modules. 0.5. Let R be a commutative ring. For every element r ∈ R, we denote by R[r −1 ] the result of inverting the element r in R, that is, the localization of R with respect to the multiplicative subset {1, r, r 2 , r 3, . . . } ⊂ R. So R[r −1 ] is an R-algebra, and in particular, an R-module. Using the telescope construction for the inductive limit, one can easily produce a two-term free resolution of the R-module R[r −1 ], so its projective dimension does not exceed 1. An R-module C is said to be r-contraadjusted if Ext1R (R[r −1 ], C) = 0. An R-module C is contraadjusted if it is r-contraadjusted for every r ∈ R. An R-module F is called very flat if Ext1R (F, C) = 0 for every contraadjusted R-module C. A somewhat more explicit or more intuitively clear description of the class of all very flat R-modules can be given using the notion of a smooth chain of submodules, or a transfinitely iterated extension. Let F be an R-module and γ be an ordinal. Suppose that for every ordinal α ≤ γ we are given a submodule Fα ⊂ F such that the following conditions are satisfied: • F0 = 0 and Fγ = F ; • one has Fα ⊂ Fβ for S all α ≤ β; • and one has Fβ = α<β Fα for all the limit ordinals β ≤ γ. Then the R-module F is said to be a transfinitely iterated extension (“in the sense of the inductive limit”) of the R-modules Fα+1 /Fα , where 0 ≤ α < γ. An R-module F is very flat if and only if it is a direct summand of a transfinitely iterated extension of (some R-modules isomorphic to) the R-modules R[r −1 ], where r ∈ R. This is a simple corollary of the main results of the paper [5] applied to the specific situation at hand (see also [20, Corollary 1.1.4]). 4  The very flat modules over commutative rings were originally defined in the preprint [20, Section 1.1] and further studied in the paper [26]. Why are they relevant to algebraic geometry? One of the simplest answers to this question is this: let U be an affine scheme, and V ⊂ U be an affine open subscheme. Then O(V ) is a very flat O(U)-module [20, Lemma 1.2.4]. A quasi-coherent sheaf F on a scheme X is said to be very flat if, for every affine open subscheme U ⊂ X, the OX (U)-module F (U) is very flat. 0.6. Now let us discuss the direct images with respect to affine morphisms. Let R −→ S be a morphism of commutative rings and G be a flat S-module. Assume that S is a flat R-module. Then G is a flat R-module. What is the analogue of this assertion for very flat modules? Let G be a very flat S-module. What do we need to know about the ring homomorphism R −→ S in order to conclude that G is a very flat R-module? The following definition provides the obvious answer: a commutative ring homomorphism R −→ S is called very flat if, for every element s ∈ S, the R-module S[s−1 ] is very flat. The naı̈ve condition that just the ring S itself should be a very flat R-module is not enough. In fact, in Example 9.7 in this paper we demonstrate an example of a commutative ring S with an element s ∈ S such that S is a free abelian group, but S[s−1 ] is not a very flat abelian group or Z-module. This discussion implies that proving that a particular ring homomorphism is very flat may be not an easy task, even in the most elementary situations. Let S = R[x] be the polynomial ring in one variable over R. How does one check that the R-module S[s−1 ] is very flat for every s ∈ S? A geometric proof of this assertion in the case when R contains a field can be found in [20, Theorem 1.7.13]. It is a complicated argument consisting of many steps. How does one prove that a finite flat morphism is very flat? Let S = R[x]/(f ) be the quotient ring of the polynomial ring R[x] by the ideal generated by a unital polynomial f (x) = xn + fn−1 xn−1 + . . . + f0 , where fi ∈ R. So S is a finitely generated free R-module of rank n. How does one check that the R-module S[s−1 ] is very flat for every s ∈ S? Our attempts to look into specific simple instances of this question led to the general theory developed in this paper. How does one prove that an étale morphism of algebraic varieties is very flat? To all of these questions, we offer one and the same answer: by applying the theorem formulated in the title of the present paper (property (VF15) below, or Main Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 9.1). 0.7. The definition of a very flat morphism of schemes in now in order. A morphism of schemes f : Y −→ X is called very flat if, for any affine open subschemes U ⊂ X and V ⊂ Y such that f (V ) ⊂ U, the OX (U)-module OY (V ) is very flat. An affine morphism f is very flat if and only if, for every affine open subscheme U ⊂ X, the morphism of rings OX (U) −→ OY (f −1 (U)) is very flat.  5  (VF11) For any very flat affine morphism f : Y −→ X, the direct image functor f∗ takes very flat quasi-coherent sheaves on Y to very flat quasi-coherent sheaves on X, that is f∗ (Y –qcohvfl ) ⊂ X–qcohvfl . S (VF12) Let f : Y −→ X be a morphism of schemes and X = α Uα be an open covering of the scheme X. Then the morphism f is very flat if and only if the morphism Uα ×X Y −→ Uα is very flat for every α. S (VF13) Let f : Y −→ X be a morphism of schemes and Y = β Vβ be an open covering of the scheme Y . Then the morphism f is very flat if and only if the morphism Vβ −→ X is very flat for every β. (VF14) Any very flat morphism of schemes f : Y −→ X is an open map between the underlying topological spaces of Y and X. One can easily check from the definitions that the composition of any two very flat morphisms is very flat. A morphism of rings R −→ S is said to be universally very flat if the induced morphism of rings T −→ S ⊗R T is very flat for every ring homomorphism R −→ T . Similarly, a morphism of schemes is said to be universally very flat if it remains very flat after any base change. (VF15) All flat morphisms of finite presentation between commutative rings are (universally) very flat. All flat morphisms (locally) of finite presentation between schemes are (universally) very flat. (VF16) Any morphism from a field to a commutative ring is universally very flat. Any morphism from a scheme to the spectrum of a field is universally very flat. In particular, the property (VF16) means that, for any commutative algebras S and T over a field k and any element w ∈ S ⊗k T , the T -module (S ⊗k T )[w −1 ] is very flat. This is the result of our Corollary 9.8. To sum up much of the preceding discussion, it follows from the properties (VF11) and (VF15) that direct images with respect to flat affine morphisms of finite presentation take very flat quasi-coherent sheaves to very flat quasi-coherent sheaves. The similar assertion, of course, holds for the flat quasi-coherent sheaves; but the conditions for the direct image to preserve the class of locally projective quasi-coherent sheaves are much more restrictive. In fact, local projectivity is not even preserved by direct images with respect to affine open embeddings. This is the reason why the proof of property (VF5) for flat or very flat quasi-coherent sheaves does not apply to locally projective ones. 0.8. It is a classical theorem in algebraic geometry that any flat morphism of schemes that is locally of finite presentation is an open map [9, Théorème 2.4.6]. Our results interpret this theorem as the combination of two implications: any flat morphism locally of finite presentation is very flat, and any very flat morphism is open. Moreover, we have a module version of (VF15) (Main Theorem 1.2), providing a similar interpretation of the assertion in [9] concerning quasi-flat morphisms. Notice that an arbitrary flat morphism of schemes is, of course, not open: it suffices to consider the morphism Spec Q −→ Spec Z. 0.10. Before we finish this introduction, let us explain the connection with contraherent cosheaves, which supplied the original motivation for introducing the notion of a very flat module in the preprint [20]. The concept of a contraherent cosheaf on a scheme is dual to that of a quasi-coherent sheaf. More specifically, a contraherent cosheaf P on a scheme X is a rule assigning to every affine open subscheme U ⊂ X an OX (U)-module P[U] and to every pair of embedded affine open subschemes V ⊂ U ⊂ X a morphism of OX (U)-modules P[V ] −→ P[U] such that for every triple of embedded affine open subschemes W ⊂ V ⊂ U ⊂ X the triangle diagram P[W ] −→ P[V ] −→ P[U] is commutative and the following two conditions are satisfied: (i) for any pair of embedded affine open subschemes V ⊂ U ⊂ X, the morphism of OX (U)-modules P[V ] −→ P[U] induces an isomorphism of OX (V )-modules HomOX (U ) (OX (V ), P[U]) ≃ P[V ];  (ii) for any pair of embedded affine open subschemes V ⊂ U ⊂ X, one has Ext1OX (U ) (OX (V ), P[U]) = 0.  The OX (U)-module OX (V ) is very flat (see Section 0.5), so its projective dimension is at most 1; that is why the groups Exti with i ≥ 2 are not mentioned in the condition (ii). Moreover, it follows that the condition (ii) is equivalent to the condition that the P[U] should be a contraadjusted OX (U)-module. In fact, the category of contraherent cosheaves on an affine scheme U is equivalent to the category of contraadjusted modules over the ring O(U). One can also consider more narrow classes of contraherent cosheaves defined by imposing stricter conditions on the OX (U)-modules P[V ]. An R-module C is called (Enochs) cotorsion [6] if Ext1R (F, C) = 0 for every flat R-module C. A contraherent cosheaf P is said to be locally cotorsion if the OX (U)-module P[U] is cotorsion for 7  every affine open subscheme U ⊂ X. A contraherent cosheaf J on a scheme X is said to be locally injective if the OX (U)-module J[U] is injective for every affine open subscheme U ⊂ X [20, Section 2.2]. 0.11. While very flat morphisms of schemes play a role in the theory of quasicoherent sheaves in that the direct image of a very flat quasi-coherent sheaf with respect to a very flat affine morphism remains very flat, they play an even more important role in the theory of contraherent cosheaves in that the very flatness of a morphism is necessary for the inverse images of contraherent cosheaves to be defined. To be more precise, there are two technical problems associated with the construction of an inverse image of a contraherent cosheaf: the nonexactness of colocalizations and the nonlocality of contraherence. To avoid the second problem, which is not relevant to our present discussion, let us assume that a morphism of schemes f : Y −→ X is coaffine, that is, for every affine open subscheme V ⊂ Y there exists an affine open subscheme U ⊂ X such that f (V ) ⊂ U. In this case, given a contraherent cosheaf P on X, one defines its inverse image f ! P on Y as the rule assigning to an affine open subscheme V ⊂ Y the OY (V )-module HomOX (U ) (OY (V ), P[U]). For this definition to work and produce a contraherent cosheaf on Y , the morphism f needs to be very flat. More narrow classes of contraherent cosheaves can be pulled back under less restrictive conditions: e. g., when P is a locally cotorsion contraherent cosheaf on X, it suffices that f be a flat morphism of schemes; and the inverse image of locally injective contraherent cosheaves can be taken with respect to an arbitrary scheme morphism [20, Section 2.3]. Locally injective contraherent cosheaves form a narrow class dual-analogous to the classes of flat or locally projective quasi-coherent sheaves. The class of locally cotorsion contraherent cosheaves is wider, and one can restrict oneself to these when working over a Noetherian or a locally Noetherian scheme, particularly if such a scheme has finite Krull dimension. (In fact, the locally cotorsion contraherent cosheaves over a locally Noetherian schemes are particuarly pleasant to work with, owing to the classification of flat cotorsion modules over Noetherian rings [6].) But over a non-Noetherian scheme locally cotorsion contraherent cosheaves are too few, and arbitrary (locally contraadjusted) contraherent cosheaves are needed. Hence the crucial importance of very flat morphisms of schemes, and consequently of the results of this paper, in the contraherent cosheaf theory. Finally, the abundance of very flat morphisms of schemes opens the perspective of developing the theory of locally contraadjusted contraherent cosheaves on stacks (e. g., described in terms of flat coverings of finite presentation); cf. Outline of the Arguments 1.1. Main theorem. Let R be a commutative ring. A commutative R-algebra S is said to be finitely presented if it can be presented as the quotient algebra R[x1 , . . . , xm ]/I of the algebra of polynomials R[x1 , . . . , xm ] in a finite number of variables with the coefficients in R by a finitely generated ideal I ⊂ R[x1 , . . . , xm ]. We refer to Section 0.5 of the Introduction above and [20, Section 1.1 and C.2] for the definitions related to very flat and contraadjusted R-modules (see also [26, Sections 2 and 5]). The following theorem is the main result of this paper. Its particular case for the Noetherian rings (cf. Main Theorem 1.3 below) was originally proposed as a conjecture in [20, Conjecture 1.7.2]. Main Theorem 1.1. Let R be a commutative ring and S be a finitely presented commutative R-algebra. Assume that S is a flat R-module. Then S is a very flat R-module. Given a ring S, an S-module is called finitely presented if it isomorphic to the cokernel of a morphism between two finitely generated free S-modules. The following generalization of Main Theorem 1.1 is useful in some applications. Main Theorem 1.2. Let R be a commutative ring, S be a finitely presented commutative R-algebra, and F be a finitely presented S-module. Assume that F is a flat R-module. Then F is a very flat R-module. 1.2. Noetherian main lemma. There are two proofs of Main Theorem 1.2 given in this paper. One of them works for Noetherian rings only. Let us formulate this assertion before explaining the key ideas behind its proof. Main Theorem 1.3. Let R be a Noetherian commutative ring, S be a finitely generated commutative R-algebra, and F be a finitely generated S-module. Assume that F is a flat R-module. Then F is a very flat R-module. The proof of Main Theorem 1.3 is based on the following main lemma. For any element r ∈ R and an R-module M, we denote by M[r −1 ] the R-module R[r −1 ]⊗R M. Main Lemma 1.4. Let R be a Noetherian commutative ring, r ∈ R be an element, and F be a flat R-module. Then the R-module F is very flat if and only if the R/rR-module F/rF is very flat and the R[r −1 ]-module F [r −1] is very flat. The “only if” assertion in Main Lemma 1.4 is easy (all extensions of scalars preserve very flatness). The “if” assertion is of key importance. The argument deducing Main Theorem 1.3 from Main Lemma 1.4 is presented is Section 2. It uses Noetherian induction and a textbook generic freeness theorem. 9  1.3. Finitely very flat main lemma. The other proof of Theorem 1.2 not only works in full generality, but also provides a stronger result. In order to formulate it, we will need the following definition. Let r1 , . . . , rm ∈ R be a finite collection of elements. We will denote it for brevity by a single letter r. Let us say that an R-module C is r-contraadjusted if it is rj -contraadjusted for every j = 1, . . . , m. An R-module F is said to be r-very flat if Ext1R (F, C) = 0 for all r-contraaadjusted R-modules C. An R-module is r-very flat if and only if it is a direct summand of a transfinitely iterated extension (in the sense of the inductive limit) of R-modules isomorphic to R or R[rj−1 ], 1 ≤ j ≤ m (see [5], [8, Corollary 6.14], or [20, Corollary 1.1.4]). Let us say that an R-module F is finitely very flat if there exists a finite collection of elements r = {r1 , . . . , rm } in R such that F is r-very flat. To repeat, it means that F is a direct summand of a module admitting a filtration indexed by an arbitrarily large ordinal with all the successive quotients belonging, up to isomorphism, to the finite set of R-modules R and R[rj−1 ]. Main Theorem 1.5. Let R be a commutative ring, S be a finitely presented commutative R-algebra, and F be a finitely presented S-module. Assume that F is a flat R-module. Then F is a finitely very flat R-module. Actually, there is more to be said about R-modules such as in Main Theorem 1.5. Any such R-module F can be presented by a countable set of generators with a countable set of relations. Using the Hill Lemma [8, (proof of) Lemma 7.10(H4)], one can deduce that F is a direct summand of an R-module admitting a filtration as above indexed by a countable ordinal. Obviously, Main Theorem 1.5 implies Main Theorem 1.2 (and Main Theorem 1.2 implies Main Theorem 1.1). The proof of Main Theorem 1.5 is based on the next main lemma. Main Lemma 1.6. Let R be a commutative ring, r ∈ R be an element, and F be a flat R-module. Then the R-module F is finitely very flat if and only if the R/rR-module F/rF is finitely very flat and the R[r −1 ]-module F [r −1 ] is finitely very flat. Once again, the “only if” assertion is easy (all extensions of scalars preserve finite very flatness). The “if” assertion is important. The argument deducing Main Theorem 1.5 from Main Lemma 1.6 is also explained in Section 2. It also uses Noetherian induction (even though the rings involved are not Noetherian!) The point is that any finitely presented algebra over a commutative ring can be obtained by an extension of scalars from a finitely presented algebra over a ring finitely generated over the integers. 1.4. Bounded torsion main lemma. Notice that Main Lemma 1.4 does not follow from Main Lemma 1.6. One of the reasons why we present two proofs of the main theorem in this paper is because we like the very flat version of the main lemma more than the finitely very flat one. 10  We do not know whether the Noetherianity condition can be dropped in Main Lemma 1.4, but it can be weakened. Given an element r in a commutative ring R and an R-module M, we say that the r-torsion in M is bounded if there exists an integer m ≥ 1 such that r n x = 0 implies r m x = 0 for all x ∈ M and n ≥ 1. The following result is provable with our methods. Main Lemma 1.7. Let R be a commutative ring and r ∈ R be an element such that the r-torsion in R is bounded. Let F be a flat R-module. Then the R-module F is very flat if and only if the R/rR-module F/rF is very flat and the R[r −1 ]-module F [r −1 ] is very flat. The condition on the element r ∈ R in Main Lemma 1.7 can be relaxed somewhat further. We refer to Remark 5.17 for the discussion. 1.5. Obtaining contraadjusted modules. Let us now say a few words about the proofs of the main lemmas. The trick is that these proofs happen on the contraadjusted rather than on the very flat side. On does not really do anything with the given flat module F . Instead, one works with an arbitrary contraadjusted R-module C (in the case of Main Lemmas 1.4 and 1.7) or with an arbitrary r-contraadjusted R-module C (in the case of Main Lemma 1.6). The contraadjusted modules are described in terms of a certain generation procedure. Given some class of R-modules E ⊂ R–mod, we produce a possibly larger class of R-modules C such that whenever one has Ext>0 R (F, E) = 0 for a given R-module >0 F and all E ∈ E, one also has ExtR (F, C) = 0 for all C ∈ C. In each of the main lemmas, in order to prove that the R-module F is (finitely) very flat, one needs to show that Ext>0 R (F, C) = 0 for every contraadjusted (or r-contraadjusted) R-module C. After all such R-modules C are known to be obtainable using a generation procedure, it remains to check that Ext>0 R (F, E) = 0 for all the R-modules E belonging to the “seed class” E. The latter is chosen in such a way that this follows pretty straightforwardly from the conditions imposed on the R-module F in the respective main lemma. The discussion of obtainable modules in this paper occupies two sections. A simpler version of obtainability sufficient for the purposes of Toy Main Lemma 1.8 (see below) is introduced in Section 3; and a more sophisticated version required for Main Lemmas 1.4, 1.6, and 1.7 is subsequently defined in Section 6. 1.6. Contramodules. Let R be a commutative ring and r ∈ R be an element. An R-module C is said to be an r-contramodule [20, Section C.2], [21], [22] if HomR (R[r −1 ], C) = 0 = Ext1R (R[r −1 ], C). This is the key technical concept used in the proofs of all the main lemmas. In the case when the r-torsion in R is bounded, r-contramodule R-modules are also known under the name of cohomologically I-adically complete modules [16, 29], where I = (r) ⊂ R denotes the principal ideal generated by the element r ∈ R. For an arbitrary element r in a commutative ring R, an R-module C is said to be r-complete if the natural map from C to its r-adic completion C −→ limn C/r n C is ←− surjective, and r-separated if this map is injective. All r-contramodule R-modules (and more generally, all r-contraadjusted R-modules) are r-complete, and all r-separated r-complete R-modules are r-contramodules, but the converse implications do not hold. The existence of nonseparated contramodules is an important technical issue which we have to deal with througout this paper (in particular, in Sections 4, 5 and 8). The category of r-contramodule R-modules is an abelian subcategory in R–mod. So is the category of R[r −1 ]-modules. The idea of the proof of Toy Main Lemma 1.8 is to show that every r-contraadjusted R-module is obtainable (in the sense discussed in Section 1.5) from r-contramodule R-modules and R[r −1 ]-modules. Then one shows that all the r-contramodule R-modules are obtainable from R/rR-modules. The proofs of Main Lemmas 1.4 and 1.7 are more difficult because one has to perform the steps staying inside the class of contraadjusted R-modules. So first one shows that every contraadjusted R-module is obtainable from a contraadjusted r-contramodule R-module and a contraadjusted R[r −1 ]-module, and then one needs to obtain a contraadjusted r-contramodule R-module out of contraadjusted R/rR-modules. It is the latter step that depends on the Noetherianity/bounded torsion assumptions on R. The homological techniques of working with contramodules that are required for this step were originally developed in [20, Sections C.2 and D.4]. These were originally intended to prepare ground for developing the theory of contraherent cosheaves of 12  contramodules over formal schemes and ind-schemes. We discuss these constructions for r-contramodule R-modules in a longish Section 5. Then we proceed to prove Main Lemmas 1.4 and 1.7 in Section 7. 1.8. Good finite subsets in a ring. There is a caveat to the discussion of r-contraadjusted R-modules in Section 1.3, and particularly to the mentioning of “an arbitrary r-contraadjusted R-module” in Section 1.5, that we should now explain. The r-contraadjusted R-module, in the context of the discussion in Section 1.5, is arbitrary, but the finite subset r ⊂ R is not. One can say that a finite subset r ⊂ R is good if, for every pair of elements r ′ and ′′ r ∈ r, there exists an element r ∈ r such that the R-algebras R[(r ′ r ′′ )−1 ] and R[r −1 ] are isomorphic to each other. In this paper, we essentially only work with good finite sets of elements r in commutative rings R. In more down-to-earth terms, this is means that, given a finite set of elements r1 , . . . , rm ∈ R, the first thing we want to do with it is to embed it into a certain larger finite Q set of elements. For every subset J ⊂ {1, . . . , m}, denote by rJ the product j∈J rj . The set of elements {rJ | J ⊂ {1, . . . , m} } is good. In particular, we can now explain how to produce the specific finite set of elements in the ring R appearing in Main Lemma 1.6. Let s = {s1 , . . . , sp } be a finite set of elements in the quotient ring R/rR such that the R/rR-module F/rF is s-very flat, and let t = {t1 , . . . , tq } be a finite set of elements in the ring of fractions R[r −1 ] such that the R[r −1 ]-module F [r −1 ] is t-very flat. Consider the collection of all elements r, s̃1 , . . . , s̃p , t̃1 , . . . , t̃q in R, and form a good finite subset r ⊂ R containing all these elements. Then the R-module F is r-very flat. Cotorsion theories in abelian categories. Let A be an abelian category and F, C ⊂ A be two classes of objects in A. We will denote by F⊥1 ⊂ A the class of all objects C ∈ A such that Ext1A (F, C) = 0 for all C ∈ A, and by ⊥1 C ⊂ A the class of all objects F ∈ A such that Ext1A (F, C) = 0 for all F ∈ A. A pair of classes of objects (F, C) in an abelian category A is called a cotorsion theory (or a cotorsion pair ) if C = F⊥1 and F = ⊥1 C (see, e. g., [27, Section 5] or [22, Section 8]). A cotorsion theory (F, C) is called complete if for every object X ∈ A there exist short exact sequences in the category A (5) (6)  0 −−→ C ′ −−→ F −−→ X −−→ 0  0 −−→ X −−→ C −−→ F ′ −−→ 0  with some objects F , F ′ ∈ F and C, C ′ ∈ C. The short exact sequences (5–6) are called approximation sequences for an object X ∈ A with respect to the cotorsion theory (F, C). The morphism F −→ X in a short exact sequence (5) is called a special F-precover of an object X, while the morphism X −→ C in a short exact sequence (6) is called a special C-preenvelope of X. Conversely, let F and C ⊂ A be two classes of objects such that Ext1A (F, C) = 0 for all F ∈ F and C ∈ C, and short exact sequences (5–6) exist for all objects X ∈ A. Assume that the classes of objects F and C are closed with respect to the passages to direct summands in A. Then the pair of classes of objects (F, C) is a (complete) cotorsion theory in A. A complete cotorsion theory (F, C) in A is said to be hereditary if one of the following equivalent conditions holds [27, Lemma 6.17]: (i) ExtiA (F, C) = 0 for all F ∈ F, C ∈ C, and i ≥ 1; (ii) Ext2A (F, C) = 0 for all F ∈ F and C ∈ C; (iii) the full subcategory F ⊂ A is closed under the passages to the kernels of epimorphisms; (iv) the full subcategory C ⊂ A is closed under the passages to the cokernels of monomorphisms. So, in a hereditary complete cotorsion theory, one has C = F⊥≥1 and F = ⊥≥1 C (in the notation of Section 3). 24  Let S ⊂ A be a class of objects. Clearly, the pair of classes F = S⊥1 and C = ⊥1 F is a cotorsion theory in A. One says that the cotorsion theory (F, C) is generated by the class of objects S ⊂ A. Any complete cotorsion theory generated by a class of objects of projective dimension not exceeding 1 in an abelian category A is hereditary (as the above condition (iv) is clearly satisfied). Moreover, in such a cotorsion theory the class C is closed under the passages to arbitrary quotient objects, while the class F consists of objects of projective dimension not exceeding 1. A classical result of the paper [5, Theorem 10] claims that, in the abelian category A = R–mod of modules over an associative ring R, any cotorsion theory generated by a set of objects is complete. Among many applications of this result, it allows to prove completeness of the following cotorsion theories in R–mod, which are of relevance to us. A left module C over an associative ring R is said to be cotorsion [6] (cf. Section 0.10) if Ext1R (F, C) = 0 for all flat left R-modules C. The pair of full subcategories (flat left R-modules, cotorsion left R-modules) forms a complete cotorsion theory in R–mod [2, Proposition 2], which is called the flat cotorsion theory. The flat cotorsion theory in R–mod is also hereditary (as the condition (iii) is satisfied). The definitions of very flat and contraadjusted modules over a commutative ring R were given in Section 0.5. The pair of full subcategories (very flat R-modules, contraadjusted R-modules) forms a complete cotorsion theory in R–mod [20, Section 1.1], which is called the very flat cotorsion theory. The very flat cotorsion theory is hereditary, because it is generated by a set of modules of projective dimension not exceeding 1 (namely, the R-modules R[r −1 ], r ∈ R). In this section, we are interested in certain (hereditary complete) cotorsion theories in the abelian category A = R–modr-ctra of r-contramodule R-modules, where R is a commutative ring and r ∈ R is an element. One thing one can say about the abelian category R–modr-ctra is that it is locally presentable, or more precisely, locally ℵ1 -presentable [24, Examples 4.1]. What we are doing here can be described as “cutting modules into pieces”. The main proposition claims that any r× -contraadjusted R-module can be cut into certain kind of pieces of simple nature. As with any such inductive transformation procedure, the trick is to make sure that we do not lose important properties along the way. The original module C is not just rm -contraadjusted; it is r× -contraadjusted. The pieces into which we cut it should remain r× -contraadjusted, or something close to that; or otherwise our induction procedure would not work. Examples and Applications Let R be a commutative ring. An R-algebra is said to be flat if it is a flat R-module. A commutative R-algebra S is called very flat if the R-module S[s−1 ] is very flat for every element s ∈ S (see the discussion in Section 0.6). 46  Corollary 9.1. Let R be a commutative ring. Then any finitely presented, flat R-algebra is very flat. Proof. Let S be a finitely presented flat R-algebra and s ∈ S be an element. Then the R-module S[s−1 ] is flat, because it is a directed inductive limit of copies of the free S-module S, which is a flat R-module by assumption. Furthermore, the R-algebra S[s−1 ] is finitely presented, since the R-algebra S is. Indeed, if S is the quotient algebra of the algebra of polynomials R[x1 , . . . , xm ] by a finitely generated ideal I, then S[s−1 ] is the quotient algebra of R[x1 , . . . , xm , z] by the finitely generated ideal J whose generators are the generators of the ideal I and the element zq(x1 , . . . , xm ) −1, where q ∈ R[x1 , . . . , xm ] is a preimage of the element s ∈ S. Applying Main Theorem 1.1, we conclude that S[s−1 ] is a very flat R-module.  Let R be a commutative ring. A commutative R-algebra S is said to be finitely very flat if the R-module S[s−1 ] is finitely very flat for every element s ∈ S. The following corollary is a stronger version of Corollary 9.1. Corollary 9.2. Let R be a commutative ring. Then any finitely presented, flat R-algebra is finitely very flat. Proof. Let S be a finitely presented flat R-algebra and s ∈ S be an element. To prove the “if”, notice the isomorphisms S ⊗R TorR i (F, N) ≃ Tori (S ⊗R F, N) ≃ TorSi (S ⊗R F, S ⊗R N), which hold for any flat R-algebra S, any R-modules F and N, and all i ≥ 0. In particular, if S is a faithfully flat R-algebra, then TorS1 (S ⊗R F, S ⊗R N) = 0 implies TorS1 (F, N) = 0.   Corollary 10.7. Let R be a Noetherian commutative ring and S be a finitely generated faithfully flat commutative R-algebra. Then an R-module F is very flat if and only if the S-module S ⊗R F is very flat. Proof. The “only if” holds by Lemma 2.2(a). To prove the “if”, assume that F is an R-module such that the S-module S ⊗R F is very flat. Then, in particular, the S-module S ⊗R F is flat, hence by Lemma 10.6 the R-module F is flat. Now we can apply Theorem 1.11 in order to conclude that the R-module F is very flat.   Corollary 10.8. Let R be a commutative ring and S be a finitely presented faithfully flat commutative R-algebra. Then an R-module F is finitely very flat if and only if the S-module S ⊗R F is finitely very flat. Proof. Similar to the proof of Corollary 10.6, using Lemma 2.2(b) (for the “only if”) and Lemma 10.6 with Theorem 1.12 (for the “if”).  Notice that Corollary 10.8 is a generalization of Lemma 10.3(b), but Corollary 10.7 does not imply Lemma 10.3(a) (because there is a Noetherianity assumption in Corollary 10.7, which was not needed in Lemma 10.3(a)).
