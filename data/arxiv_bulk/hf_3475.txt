1  A Mismatched Joint Source-Channel Coding Perspective of Probabilistic Amplitude Shaping: Achievable Rates and Error Exponents Rana Ali Amjad  arXiv:1802.05973v1 [cs.IT] 16 Feb 2018  Abstract Probabilistic Amplitude Shaping (PAS) is a novel method of reliable commmunication over channels with non-uniform capacity achieving input distribution. The encoder of PAS is a concatenation of a distribution matcher with systematic encoding of a Forward Error Correction (FEC) code whereas the PAS decoder is a concatenation of a mismatched FEC decoder and dematcher. In recent works PAS has been shown to close the shaping gap to Shannon capacity for discrete input AWGN channel for a wide range of SNR values. In this paper we modify the classical joint source-channel coding setup to include the salient features of PAS and we derive the Gallager style error exponent and consequently achievable rates for this setup.  I. I NTRODUCTION In his seminal paper [1], Shannon showed that it is not possible to communicate reliably over a noisy channel at rates higher than channel capacity. He further showed that by generating codebook randomly according to the capacity achieving distribution denoted by PX ∗ , one could achieve any rates below the channel capacity. Shamai et.al. showed that, for unique PX ∗ and under some regularity conditions, the empirical distribution of blocks of k channel input symbols in the codebook should be close to the product distribution PXk ∗ for nay fixed k as the blocklength becomes sufficiently large. We also know that for AWGN channel, using uniformly distributed equidistant input signal points leads to a gap of upto 1.53 dB (at high SNR) from the channel capacity. This gap is known as the shaping gap. Various methods have been proposed to close this gap, see [2] for a literature review on this topic. These methods either suffer from significant increase in complexity or don’t offer much gain in practical settings. PAS has been proposed recently by Böcherer et. al to solve this problem [2]. It has been applied in various communication scenarios including [3] and [4] showing significant rate gains as compared to the previously known state-of-the-art techniques. Furthermore in [2] the authors claim that PAS also provides a flexible and low complexity mechanism to adapt rates to the changing channel conditions. The aim of this work is to adapt the classical joint source-channel coding setup to include salient features of PAS i.e. the systematic encoding of a non uniform source and the mismatched decoding. Using this modified setup we will do a Gallager style error exponent analysis using random coding [5] and will show that PAS can achieve channel capacity for a class of Discrete Input Memoryless Channels (DIMC). The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II we introduce the required definitions and then review the key aspects of Gallager’s coding theorem for non-uniform source. In Sec. II-C we introduce the PAS in more detail. In Sec. III and Sec. IV we look at two joint source channel coding scenarios, each having one modification as compared to the original setup by Gallager [5]. Sec. III deals the setup where we use a systematic encoder for transmitting messages from a possibly non uniform Discrete Memoryless Source (DMS) over a noisy channel. Sec. IV deals with the setup where we have a mismatched MAP channel decoder. Unlike the previous works on mismatched decoding [6], [7] where the authors dealt with channel mismatch and/or complexity constraints on the decoder, we look at mismatch of source statistics. In Sec. V we combine the two setups from Sec. III and Sec. IV to discuss error exponent and achievable rates for PAS.  2  W  fn  Xn  PY |X  Yn  gn  Ŵ  Fig. 1: Classical Joint-Source Channel Coding Setup II. P RELIMINARIES Definition 1 (Type of a Sequence). Probabilistic Amplitude Shaping The distribution which maximizes the R.H.S of (15) is known as capacity achieving distribution and is denoted by PX ∗ , i.e., PX ∗ = argmax I (X; Y )  (16)  PX  PX ∗ may or may not be unique. In case of a unique PX ∗ , for any sequence of codes to be capacity achieving the k th empirical distribution of the codebook (under certain regularity conditions) should be ”close” to the product distribution PXk ∗ for any fixed k as blocklength n becomes sufficiently large [10]. Furthermore many practically important channels have non-uniform capacity achieving distributions. One such example is AWGN channel with average input power constraint. For this channel the PX ∗ is gaussian distribution with zero mean. When using a finite input constellation to communicate over the AWGN channel, as is the in any practical system, there is a gap of 1.53 dB (at high SNR) to channel capacity if one uses uniformly spaced and uniformly distributed input constellation. This gap is known as the shaping gap. In order to close this gap the discrete input constellation should mimic the capacity achieving distribution of the discretized input channel. This is called signal shaping. Two methods to do this are geometric shaping and probabilistic shaping. Geometric shaping has a non-uniformly spaced grid of constellation points with equiprobable symbols whereas probabilistic shaping has a uniformly spaced grid with unequal symbol probabilities. Both techniques have been shown to close the shaping gap for AWGN channel [11], [12]. Similar arguments hold for other DIMCs with non-uniform PX ∗ which motivates us to do signal shaping for better achievable rates. Various methods have been proposed for signal shaping including many-to-one mapping [9] and trellis shaping [13] in the literature but these methods suffer from significant increase in the decoding complexity because of the interplay between signal shaping and FEC. PAS is a novel method of probabilistic shaping recently introduced by Böcherer et.al [2]. PAS decouples the task of shaping from FEC, hence leading to a minimal increase in complexity as claimed by Böcherer et.al [2]. They also claim that this seperation provides the flexibilty of using PAS with off the shelf FEC codes as well as an easy and flexible mechanism to adapt the rate according to the changing channel conditions. These characteristics make PAS a highly desirable signal shaping mechanism in practice. PAS scheme is shown in Fig. 2. In the first step the distribution matcher invertibly maps the source message W to a sequence An . The aim of the distribution matcher is to have the output statistics close to PAn . The fundamental limits of distribution matching have been discussed in [15] and the optimal fixed length distribution matcher was discussed in [16]. In [17] Schulte et.al proposed CCDM, a practical distibution matcher that produces output sequences from a chosen type set. The output An of the distribution matcher is then fed to a systematic FEC code represented by fnsys . Internally An is fed to a parity generator hn which generates parity symbols S n . S n and An are then fed to a modulator (represented by ⊗) to create channel input X n s.t. Xi = Ai Si , where Ai Si represents the concatenation of Ai and Si . In the rest of the paper we sometimes write xn = (an , sn ) as a shorthand notation for xi = ai si for all i = 1 . . . n. At the receiver PAS uses a mismatched FEC decoder to recover the sequence Ân . The mismatched decoder ignores the actual apriori statistics of An and assumes the output of the distribution matcher to be from a Discrete Memoryless Source (DMS) PA . This assumption is made to reduce the complexity of the decoder and admits symbol metric decoding in our framework. Ân is then fed to the de-matcher which recovers Ŵ . For theoretical analysis of PAS we will consider the setup shown in Fig. 3. Instead of including the distribution matcher for analysis, it suffices to consider the source message to be An with source probability distribution Q that matches the output of a distribution matcher. We will also restrict ourselves to DIMCs which have input alphabet X that can be represented in the form A × S and to input distributions of the form PX = PA PS where PA is any arbitrary distribution over A and PS is uniform over S unless stated otherwise. We restrict ourselves to uniform PS because linear FEC codes in practice generate parity bits by modulo-2 sums of multiple source bits, leading to more uniform marginal distributions of the parity bits.
