This set-up covers a broad class of sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods, which are widely-used in applied statistics and cognate disciplines across a range of domains. We consider the genealogical tree embedded into such particle systems by the resampling operation, and identify conditions, as well as an appropriate time-scaling, under which it converges to the Kingman coalescent in the infinite system size limit in the sense of finite-dimensional distributions. This makes the plethora of distributional results known for the Kingman coalescent available for analysis of SMC algorithms, which we illustrate by characterising the limiting mean and variance of the tree height, as well as of the total branch length of the tree. It also greatly improves the tractability of genealogies of SMC methods, which are known to be closely connected to the performance of these algorithms. The conditions which we require to prove convergence are strong, but we demonstrate by simulation that they do not appear to be necessary.  1  Introduction  Interacting particle systems constitute a broad class of stochastic models for phenomena in various disciplines including physics, engineering, biology and finance. Particularly prominent settings touching upon all of these fields are numerical approximation schemes for non-linear filtering and mean field approximation of Feynman-Kac flows, in which such systems describe a widely-used class of algorithms known under names which include particle filters, particle methods and sequential Monte Carlo. For additional background on these models and their applications, the interested reader is directed to the monograph [Del04], as well as the review article [DJ11]. Central to these applications is the notion of a weighted system of particles 1  evolving in discrete time in a correlated fashion, as we will outline below. Correlations between particles arise out of a stochastic selection mechanism known as resampling, in which particles with high weight are typically replicated while those with low weight vanish, giving rise to an embedded genealogy. The contribution of this paper is to show that these genealogies converge to the coalescent, first described in Kingman’s seminal work [Kin82], in the limit of infinite system size under certain conditions and an appropriate rescaling of time, as well as to describe ways in which information about the limiting genealogy can be used to approximately characterize particle systems of finite size in applications. Particle filters are especially well suited to multidimensional settings in which the density being integrated corresponds to the law of a latent, discrete-time Markov process conditioned upon a sequence of observations, such as hidden Markov models or more general state space models. (1:N ) Tracing back the ancestor indices a0:(t−1) results in a coalescing forest of lineages, ultimately forming a tree once the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of all particles is reached, provided that happens before reaching the initial time 0. The main result of this manuscript (Theorem 1) is to show that, under certain conditions and under an appropriate rescaling, certain functionals of this genealogy (namely those depending upon finite numbers of leaves) converge to corresponding functionals of the Kingman coalescent [Kin82] as N → ∞. This makes the plethora of distributional results that are known for the Kingman coalescent available for the study of SMC algorithms. In particular, we show that the expected height of the genealogy — the number of steps from the leaves to the MRCA — scales linearly in N for any finite number of leaves. This compares to an N log N upper bound obtained in [JMR15] to estimate the memory cost of storing the genealogy of all N leaves, and proves that their bound is tight up to a log N correction. We also provide scaling expressions for the variance of the tree height, as well as the mean and variance of the total branch length of the genealogy, again for any finite number of leaves (see Corollary 1). The conditions for convergence of genealogies to the Kingman coalescent turn out to be cumbersome in general (see (3) and (4)), but in the SMC context they are satisfied, at least when multinomial resampling is employed, under the strong but standard condition (18) which requires the potential function to be bounded away from both zero and infinity. This condition is used to control the fluctuations of family sizes; we expect that it will be possible to relax it, and simulation results presented below also suggest that it is not necessary. In addition to impacting on memory consumption, the loss of lineages due to mergers into common ancestors results in a phenomenon known as path degeneracy in SMC algorithms. In brief, quantities of interest depending on times t < T will be estimated using fewer than N lineages, resulting in estimators with high variance. Path degeneracy can be reduced slightly by resampling less frequently, but this results in increased variance of estimators across time. The extreme case of no resampling results in no path degeneracy, but is well-known to yield estimators whose variances increase exponentially in T in most cases: a phenomenon known as weight degeneracy [KLW94]. Intermediate strategies balance these two difficulties [LC95, Section 4.2]. Our limiting genealogies are the only method of which we are aware that can provide a priori estimates of path degeneracy, and thus represent an important tool in the design task of minimising both types of degeneracy simultaneously. This is particularly important in the conditional SMC update in the particle Gibbs algorithm [ADH10], where retaining multiple trajectories across a fixed time window is essential for good performance. 4  Genealogies of SMC algorithms have also been utilised for various purposes in [DM01, DG05, DM07, DMPR09, LW15]; see also [Del04, Section 3.4] and [Del13, Chapter 15]. Our aim of describing the asymptotic genealogical process explicitly is new in the SMC setting, and has the potential to build on on any of these earlier works, as well as generate new ones in the interacting particle system and sequential Monte Carlo fields. In addition, our work demonstrates that the domain of attraction of Kingman’s coalescent includes particle systems with non-Markovian prelimiting genealogies, in contrast with earlier results in which Markovianity has been assumed [Möh98, Theorem 1]. This extension is significant since genealogies of Markov interacting particle systems are not Markov processes in general. The rest of this paper is structured as follows. In Section 2 we state and prove our main result on the convergence of genealogies of interacting particle systems to the Kingman coalescent. Section 3 presents a numerical simulation illustrating finite sample convergence strictly outside the scope of our technical assumptions, and Section 4 concludes with a discussion.  2  The convergence theorem  It will be convenient to express our system in reverse time by denoting the initial time in Algorithm 1 as −T and the terminal time as 0, and to describe the genealogy in terms of (n,N ) −T a partition-valued, reverse-time family of processes {Gt }t=0 indexed by n ≤ N , where n denotes the number of observed particles in a system with N particles in total. Particle indices (n,N ) i 6= j ∈ {1, . . . , n} belong to the same block in partition Gt if and only if particles i and j at (n,N ) time 0 have a common ancestor at time t ≤ −1. The initial condition is G0 = {{1}, . . . , {n}}, i.e. the singleton partition corresponding to a forest of n unmerged particles. Boundary problems will be avoided by ensuring that T → ∞ in our rescaled system as N → ∞. Ancestral modelling is also a powerful tool in population genetics, where the genealogical tree is often viewed as missing data to be imputed, or an object of inference in its own right. A common large population limit is given by the Kingman n-coalescent [Kin82], which is also a random partition-valued stochastic process evolving in reverse time. The initial condition is the singleton partition {{1}, . . . , {n}}. Each pair of blocks then merges to a common ancestor independently at rate 1, forming a death process on the total number of blocks with rate k2 when there are k blocks. The process terminates once it hits the MRCA, i.e. the trivial partition {{1, . . . , n}}, which can then be connected back to time −∞ while undergoing no dynamics. For a more comprehensive introduction to coalescent processes and their use in population genetics, we direct the interested reader to [Wak09]. sense of finite-dimensional distributions, to the Kingman coalescent under a suitable rescaling of time, and certain assumptions. Thus, we are able to use the extensive catalogue of distributional facts known about the Kingman coalescent to characterise SMC genealogies as well. We illustrated this in Corollary 1 by obtaining scaling results for the first two moments of the total tree height and total branch length of the genealogical tree. Asymptotic expressions for many other quantities, such as the probability of retaining at least two branches for a time window of a desired length, can also be easily obtained. Our method is the only tool for a priori estimation of path degeneracy of which we are aware, and represents an important step towards practical guidelines for simultaneous minimisation of path degeneracy and weight degeneracy. 21  0.06  Var(−T/N)  0.08  o multinomial + residual x stratified * systematic  * x x  0.04  0.06  0.08  o multinomial + residual x stratified * systematic  0.04  Var(−T/N)  0.10  n=8  0.10  n=2  x* + ●  +* x●  x* +  x +*  ●  ●  ● ●  0.02  0.02  + +* x* + x* + ● x● + *  x* +  x* +  ●  ●  * + x●  x * + ●  x + * ●  0.00  0.00  ●  4000  6000  0.08 Var(−T/N)  ●  x + *  ●  ●  x  ●  x* +  x +*  x + *  ●  ●  0.00  * + ● x  4000  6000  8000  0  n = 512  n = 2048  0.08 Var(−T/N)  x +*  x +*  ●  o multinomial + residual x stratified * systematic  * + ● x  0.02  * + ● x  ●  8000  0.06  0.08 0.06 0.04 0.02  x* +  6000  0.10  N  x  ●  4000  N  o multinomial + residual x stratified * systematic  + *  2000  0.04  2000  0.10  0  x +*  x +*  ●  ●  0.00  ●  0.00  Var(−T/N)  x * +  ●  0.00  ●  * + x●  o multinomial + residual x stratified * systematic  + x* ● * + ● 0.02  x* +  8000  0.06  0.08  ●  x +*  6000  0.10  n = 128  0.06  x* +  4000  n = 32  x + *  2000  N  x* +  ●  0.02  0  o multinomial + residual x stratified * systematic  0.04  Var(−T/N)  8000  N  0.04  2000  0.10  0  0  2000  4000  6000  8000  0  N  2000  4000  6000  8000  N  Figure 4: Variance profile of tree height for N ≥ n leaves for different values of n.  22  The assumptions used to obtain our convergence statement are restrictive, but give rise to the first result of the kind outlined above. Essentially, they require a compact state space and multinomial resampling. However, the simulation study performed in Section 3 suggests that neither of these assumptions is necessary: our example did not satisfy (4), and a range of resampling methods in addition to multinomial were observed to fit the scalings predicted by Corollary 1. Our simulations also confirmed that these scalings hold non-asymptotically. We believe that there is scope to substantially relax these assumptions if more subtle arguments can be employed to control the variability of family sizes. A different perspective on Theorem 1 is that it demonstrates that the domain of attraction of the Kingman coalescent includes non-Markovian genealogies. Coalescent processes are wellestablished models in population genetics, where family sizes are typically independent between time steps, and thus give rise to Markovian genealogies. In such settings, the Kingman coalescent has been shown to be the limiting process corresponding to a remarkably broad class of models [Möh98, Theorem 1]. Our Theorem 1 generalises such results further by demonstrating that the Kingman coalescent can be obtained as the limiting process of non-Markovian genealogies as well. An immediate consequence is improved tractability of genealogies from models of evolution with random but inherited fecundity, which would also violate the standard assumption of Markovian genealogies. This perspective also suggests an avenue of future research into establishing stronger forms of convergence akin to [Möh99, Theorem 3.1].
