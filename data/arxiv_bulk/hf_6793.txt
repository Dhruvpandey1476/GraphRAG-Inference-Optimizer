Posterior Sampling for Large Scale Reinforcement Learning  arXiv:1711.07979v2 [cs.LG] 6 Dec 2017  Georgios Theocharous theochar@adobe.com  Zheng Wen zwen@adobe.com  Abstract  Posterior sampling for reinforcement learning (PSRL) is a popular algorithm for learning to control an unknown Markov decision process (MDP). PSRL maintains a distribution over MDP parameters and in an episodic fashion samples MDP parameters, computes the optimal policy for them and executes it. A special case of PSRL is where at the end of each episode the MDP resets to the initial state distribution. Extensions of this idea to general MDPs without state resetting has so far produced non-practical algorithms and in some cases buggy theoretical analysis. This is due to the difficulty of analyzing regret under episode switching schedules that depend on random variables of the true underlying model. We propose a solution to this problem that involves using a deterministic, model-independent episode switching schedule, and establish a Bayes regret bound under mild assumptions. Our algorithm termed deterministic schedule PSRL (DS-PSRL) is efficient in terms of time, sample, and space complexity. Our result is more generally applicable to continuous state action problems. We demonstrate how this algorithm is well suited for sequential recommendation problems such as points of interest (POI). We derive a general procedure for parameterizing the underlying MDPs, to create action condition dynamics from passive data, that do not contain actions. We prove that such parameterization satisfies the assumptions of our analysis.  Yasin Abbasi-Yadkori abbasiya@adobe.com  1  Nikos Vlassis nvlassis@netflix.com  Introduction  Reinforcement learning (RL) deals with the problem of how an agent should learn to maximize net reward or minimize net cost through repeated interaction with an environment [16]. Often the environment can be modeled as a Markov decision process (MDP). If the MDP model is known, the optimal behavior of the agent can be computed by dynamic programming. If the MDP is unknown, the learner needs to balance exploration of the environment to learn its structure versus exploitation of what is currently known. Thompson sampling [18], or posterior sampling for reinforcement learning (PSRL), is a conceptually simple approach to deal with unknown MDPs [11, 15]. PSRL begins with a prior distribution over the MDP model parameters (transitions and/or rewards) and typically works in episodes. At the start of each episode, an MDP model is sampled from the posterior belief and the agent follows the policy that is optimal for that sampled MDP until the end of the episode. The posterior is updated at the end of every episode based on the observed actions, states, and rewards. A special case of MDP under which PSRL has been recently extensively studied is MDP with state resetting, either explicitly or implicitly. Specifically, in [11, 12] the considered MDPs are assumed to have fixed-length episodes, and at the end of each episode the MDP’s state is reset according to a fixed state distribution. In [7], there is an assumption that the environment is ergodic and that there exists a recurrent state under any policy. Both [11, 12] and [7] have developed variants of PSRL algorithms under their respective assumptions, as well as state-of-the-art regret bounds, Bayesian in [11, 12] and Frequentist in [7]. However, many real-world problems are of a continuing and non-resetting nature. Consider for instance, a points of interest (POI) recommendation system where the MDP policies are recommendation plans for users visiting various POIs over the course of a day [10, 19]. This type of domains do not have the luxury of state resetting, and the agent needs to learn to act, without necessarily revisiting states. Extensions of this idea  Posterior Sampling for Large Scale Reinforcement Learning  to general MDPs without state resetting has so far produced non-practical algorithms and in some cases buggy theoretical analysis. This is due to the difficulty of analyzing regret under policy switching schedules that depend on various dynamic statistics produced by the true underlying model (e.g., doubling the visitations of state and action pairs and uncertainty reduction of the parameters). Next we summarize the literature for this general case PSRL. The earliest such general case was analyzed as Bayes regret in a ‘lazy’ PSRL algorithm [1]. In this approach a new model is sampled, and a new policy is computed from it, every time the uncertainty over the underlying model is sufficiently reduced; however, the corresponding analysis was shown to contain a gap [13]. A recent general case PSRL algorithm with Bayes regret analysis was proposed in [14]. At the beginning of each episode, the algorithm generates a sample from the posterior distribution over the unknown model parameters. It then follows the optimal stationary policy for the sampled model for the rest of the episode. The duration of each episode is dynamically determined by two stopping criteria. A new episode starts either when the length of the current episode exceeds the previous length by one, or when the number of visits to any √ state-action pair is doubled. In such non-parameterized case, there are several other modern reinforcement learning algorithms, such as UCRL2 [8], REGAL [3], and R-max [5], which learn MDPs using the well-known ‘optimism under uncertainty’ principle. In these approaches a confidence interval is maintained for each state-action pair, and observing a particular state transition and reward provides information for only that state and action. Such approaches are inefficient in cases where the whole structure of the MDP can be determined with a few parameters. Despite the elegant regret bounds for the general case PSRL algorithms developed in [2, 14], both of them focus on tabular reinforcement learning and hence are sample inefficient for many practical problems with exponentially large or even continuous state/action spaces. On the other hand, in many practical RL problems, the MDPs are parameterized in the sense that system dynamics and reward/loss functions are assumed to lie in a known parametrized low-dimensional manifold [7]. Such model parameterization (i.e. model generalization) allows researchers to develop sample efficient algorithms for large-scale RL problems. Our paper belongs to this line of research. The challenge for a PSRL style algorithm in this setting is how to decide when to switch policies. Changing the policy too often may lead to oscillating behavior. For example, if the switching time is smaller than the corresponding mixing time of the process under the optimal policy, the agent may never be able to learn the optimal policy. On the other hand, if the switching time is much larger than the timeframe of the optimal policy then the agent may be overly wasteful in not updating its policy as data arrives. To our knowledge, there are three PSRL algorithms reported in the literature applicable to non-episodic problems as described in the introduction. The first is the Lazy-PSRL algorithm [1]. Lazy-PSRL uses a dynamic strategy of model resampling rather than using fixed episode lengths. The algorithm resamples new MDP parameters every time there is sufficient reduction in the variance of the distribution of the parameters. Nonetheless, the analysis of Lazy-PSRL contained a delicate error in the way it applied Lemma 1, as pointed out in [13]. The other two approaches are [2, 14], which have sound theoretical bounds, but are limited to the tabular RL case. Hence, they are impractical for large-scale problems.  Posterior Sampling for Large Scale Reinforcement Learning  4  The proposed algorithm: Deterministic schedule PSRL  In this section, we propose a PSRL algorithm with a deterministic policy update schedule. To measure the performance of our algorithm we use Bayes regret RT defined in Equation 1. The slower the regret grows, the closer is the performance of the learner to that of an optimal policy. If the growth rate of RT is sublinear (RT = o(T )), the average loss per time step will converge to the optimal average loss as T gets large, and in this sense we can say that the algorithm is asymptotically optimal. Our main result shows that, under certain conditions, the construction of such asymptotically optimal policies can be reduced to efficiently sampling from the posterior of θ∗ and solving classical (non-Bayesian) optimal control problems. The algorithm has similar regret to state-of-the-art. However, our result is more generally applicable to continuous state-action problems; when dynamics of the system is parameterized by a small number of variables, our regret scales with the square root of the number of parameters and is independent of the number of states. 1 The data and pre-processing algorithms are publicly available on https://github.com/arongdari/flickr-photo  In addition, our algorithm is practical. The algorithm provides for generalization, and uses a deterministic policy switching schedule of logarithmic order, which is independent from the true model of the world. This leads to efficiency in sample, space and time complexities. The algorithm is demonstrated in two applications. For the synthetic problem we demonstrated the ability of the algorithm to solve problems that require multistep exploration. For the real world problem on POI recommendations we showed how to build a sequential recommendation system from passive data with action parameterization and demonstrated its convergence and advantages over greedy approaches.
