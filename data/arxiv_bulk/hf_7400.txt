arXiv:1610.05365v1 [math.GR] 17 Oct 2016  Structure of almost Abelian Lie algebras Zhirayr Avetisyan Department of Mathematics, UCL March 28, 2018  Abstract An almost Abelian Lie algebra is a non-Abelian Lie algebra with a codimension 1 Abelian subalgebra. It is known that such an algebra necessarily has a codimension 1 Abelian ideal. Almost Abelian Lie algebras are perhaps the next simplest Lie algebras after Abelian Lie algebras. The present exposition studies the structure and important algebraic properties of almost Abelian Lie algebras of arbitrary dimension over any field of scalars.  1  Introduction  In our definition an almost Abelian Lie algebra is a non-Abelian Lie algebra L over a field F which has a codimension 1 Abelian subalgebra. It was shown in [4] (Proposition 3.1) that such an algebra necessarily contains a codimension 1 Abelian ideal. The result is announced for fields F of characteristic zero and in finite dimensions, but in fact the proof works with minor adaptations in the most general case. Often almost Abelian Lie algebras are defined as those having a codimension 1 Abelian ideal, but in view of the result above we find it more convenient to start with a codimension 1 subalgebra. There is certain controversy regarding the notion of an almost Abelian Lie algebra in the literature. Some authors (e.g., [11], [8], [13]) define an almost Abelian Lie algebra as a Lie algebra for which there exists a basis {e0 , ei }, i = 1, ..., n such that [e0 , ei ] = ei and [ei , ej ] = 0 for all i, i.e., ade0 = 1. This definition is too strict in that it misses all other possibilities for the operator ade0 not similar to identity. On the other hand, some sources prefer to include Abelian Lie algebras in the class of almost Abelian Lie algebras (e.g., [11],[17]), but we prefer not to do so because of principal structural differences between the two.  1  Probably the best motivation to study almost Abelian Lie algebras comes from the theory of integrable systems and PDE. Let (C ∞ (M ), {, }) be a Poisson algebra over an n-dimensional manifold M , and let H ∈ C ∞ (M ) be a Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian system evolving according to H is Liouville integrable if H is contained in an Abelian subalgebra of (C ∞ (M ), {, }) (considered as a Lie algebra) generated by at least n functionally independent integrals of motion. In particular, if H is invariant under the action of a Lie algebra L of Noether symmetries which has an at least n − 1-dimensional Abelian subalgebra then the system is integrable in Noether integrals. It follows that every Hamiltonian system invariant under a transitively acting (i.e., dim L = n and complete) almost Abelian Lie algebra of Noether symmetries is integrable in Noether integrals. For instance, every invariant Hamiltonian system on an almost Abelian Lie group (i.e., a Lie group with an almost Abelian Lie algebra) is integrable in Noether integrals. Integrability in Noether integrals may be labelled simplistic, but the class of invariant systems on almost Abelian Lie groups is sufficiently diverse to demonstrate non-trivial phenomena as non-commutativity, non-unimodularity and curvature, which can be studied in a completely explicit manner. Another way an almost Abelian Lie algebra arises is from a linear dynamical system. Let F be a field, and V an F-vector space. A discrete linear dynamical system is an equation of the form v(n + 1) = Tv(n),  v : N0 7→ V,  where T ∈ EndF (V) is a linear operator. To give our dynamical system a Hamiltonian flavour consider the extended F-vector space L = FH ⊕F V with a distinguished element H ∈ L called the Hamiltonian. Make L into a Lie algebra by setting [u, v] = 0,  [H, v] = adH v = Tv,  ∀v, u ∈ V.  Now if T 6= 0 then L is an almost Abelian Lie algebra that encodes the dynamics of the original system (if T = 0 then L is Abelian and the dynamics is trivial), v(n + 1) = [H, v(n)]. If the vector space V is endowed with a topology then the continuous counterpart of the system  2  can be considered as well, v.(t) = [H, v(t)]. t. Thus an almost Abelian Lie algebra is also a stationary linear dynamical system, and the study of its algebraic properties yields to the understanding of the dynamics. Again, linear dynamical systems may be considered a textbook subject as they are usually solved in terms of matrix algebra. But things are not as trivial when the operator T is not diagonalizable, or V is infinite dimensional, or when F is neither R nor C. Matrix tools such as determinants, traces and transpositions need not exist. Moreover, even advanced methods like canonical forms (e.g., Jordan or Frobenius) become non-trivial issues and need not exist. No reference can be made to the spectral theory of Hilbert space operators because V is merely a vector space. And even in the simplest case of a real or complex finite dimensional diagonalizable matrix T many algebraic aspects need to be described explicitly and in a systematic manner. The smallest (in terms of dimensions) almost Abelian Lie algebra is the only (up to isomorphism) non-Abelian Lie algebra of dimension 2, which is the Lie algebra ax+bF of the group of affine transformations on the real line. This algebra can be described in a basis {e0 , e1 } with relations [e0 , e1 ] = e1 , i.e., ade0 = 1. In dimension 3 the almost Abelian Lie algebras are the non-Abelian solvable Bianchi algebras Bi(II)-Bi(VII) (Bi(I) is Abelian and Bi(VIII) and Bi(IX) are simple, so we will not consider them here) which were first classified for F = R by Bianchi [2] (see [9] for a more modern and elegant approach). The classification corresponds to the similarity classes of 2 × 2 matrices ade0 . The most prominent of them is the nilpotent Heisenberg algebra HF which arises from the canonical commutation relations in quantum mechanics, e0 =  ∂ , ∂x  e1 = 1,  e2 = x,  [e0 , e2 ] = e1 ,  [e0 , e1 ] = [e1 , e2 ] = 0.  Thorough studies of the Heisenberg algebra and the corresponding Lie group can be found, for instance, in [6] and [19]. The remaining solvable Bianchi algebras are mainly known in the context of cosmology. They arise as the algebras of geometric symmetries in homogeneous anisotropic cosmological models ([5], [14], [16], [18] and many others). More details on the relevance of Bianchi groups in cosmology can be found in [1] and references therein. Another very interesting application of 3-dimensional solvable Lie algebras is crystallography, where these algebras and their Lie groups represent the spatial symmetries of anisotropic crystals (see, for instance, [15] and references therein). In this paper we consider almost Abelian Lie algebras L of any dimension dimF L = 1 + ℵ over any field F, so that the codimension 1 Abelian ideal has dimension ℵ. Note that we identify  3  index sets with their cardinalities, therefore we use the symbol ℵ to denote both the cardinality of a Hamel basis in a vector space and an actual index set of that cardinality. In order to deal with this generality certain precautions have been taken in the methodology. No integers other than 0 and ±1 are assumed to be contained in F a priory in order to avoid a potential conflict with the characteristic of F. No reference is made to polynomial algebraic and spectral methods such as eigenvalues or factorizations so that algebraic and topological aspects of F such as algebraic extensions or topological closedness are irrelevant. And of course, no dimensional or matrix arguments such as rank-nullity or canonical forms can be used in this generality. This may have made some otherwise trivial facts unexpectedly tricky, but the matrix intuition mostly remains valid. Some structures as homomorphisms become somewhat richer in infinite dimensions. The paper is structured as follows. First in section 2 we introduce notations by reminding some notions and facts from the elementary Lie theory which are used in the body. This makes the paper essentially self-contained with little reference to the state-of-the-art in the field, and thereby more accessible to non-specialists. Then in section 3 we define almost Abelian Lie algebras, discuss their subalgebras and ideals, and decompose them into an indecomposable core and a central extension. Further in section 4 we describe homomorphisms between and automorphisms of almost Abelian Lie algebras which then naturally leads to a classification up to isomorphism. Afterwards in section 5 we describe derivations and Lie orthogonal operators on almost Abelian Lie algebras. Finally in section 6 we study the centre of the universal enveloping algebra of an almost Abelian Lie algebra.  2  Lie algebras  Lie algebras will be the main subject of this exposition, so let us collect some definitions, notations and facts including proofs where the latter are particularly simple. For a thorough exposition of Lie algebras and Lie groups we refer to the all time classical monograph by Knapp [12]. A Lie algebra is a vector space L (over a field F) along with an antisymmetric bilinear map [, ] : L ⊗ L 7→ L called Lie brackets which satisfy the so called Jacobi identity, [X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y ] = 0,  ∀X, Y, Z ∈ L.  A Lie subalgebra is a subspace L0 ⊂ L such that [L0 , L0 ] ⊂ L0 . An F-linear map U : L1 7→ L2  4  between two Lie algebras L1 and L2 with Lie brackets [, ]1 and [, ]2 , respectively, is called a homomorphism if U[X, Y ]1 = [UX, UY ]2 ,  ∀X, Y ∈ L1 .  If in addition U is bijective then it is called an isomorphism, and the two Lie algebras are called isomorphic, L1 ≃ L2 . A Lie subalgebra I ⊂ L is an ideal if [I, L] ⊂ I. Proposition 1 For a Lie algebra L and an ideal I ⊂ L, the quotient space L/I with Lie brackets induced by the quotient map qI : L 7→ L/I is a Lie algebra, and qI is a Lie algebra homomorphism onto. Proof: That I is an ideal implies [X + I, Y + I] ⊂ [X, Y ] + I,  ∀X, Y ∈ L.  The rest is straightforward.  The following is referred to as the First Isomorphism Theorem for Lie algebras. Theorem 1 For a Lie algebra homomorphism U : L1 7→ L2 between two Lie algebras L1 and L2 the following assertions hold: • The image UL1 ⊂ L2 is a Lie subalgebra • The kernel ker U ⊂ L1 is an ideal • The image is isomorphic to the quotient, UL1 ≃ L1 / ker U Proof: For every X, Y ∈ L1 we have [X, Y ] ∈ L1 and [UX, UY ] = U[X, Y ] ∈ UL1 showing that UL1 is a Lie subalgebra. If in addition Y ∈ ker U then U[X, Y ] = [UX, UY ] = 0 implying that [X, Y ] ∈ ker U, i.e., ker U is an ideal. If we now define a map T : L1 / ker U 7→ UL1 by setting T(X + ker U) = UX, ∀X ∈ L1 , we can easily verify that T is a Lie algebra isomorphism.  If I ⊂ L is an ideal and if there exists a Lie algebra homomorphism τ : L/I 7→ L such that q ◦ τ = 1 then τ (L/I) ⊂ L is a Lie subalgebra of L which is isomorphic to L/I. In this case we write L/I ⊂ L and L = L/I ⋊ I and say that L is the semidirect product of L/I and I. A representation ρ of a Lie algebra L on a vector space W is a Lie algebra homomorphism ρ : L 7→ EndF (W) where the Lie brackets in the endomorphism ring are given by the ordinary  5  commutator, [V, U] = VU − UV,  ∀V, U ∈ EndF (W).  A representation is called faithful if it is injective. For every Lie algebra L the adjoint representation ad : L 7→ EndF (L) is given by adX Y = [X, Y ],  ∀X, Y ∈ L.  That it is indeed a representation can be shown using the Jacobi identity above. The adjoint representation need not be faithful. A Lie algebra L is called decomposable if it decomposes into a direct sum L = L1 ⊕ L2 of two commuting non-trivial Lie subalgebras L1 6= 0 and L2 6= 0. Otherwise L is called indecomposable. A Lie algebra L is called Abelian if [X, Y ] = 0 for all X, Y ∈ L. It is called (N -step) nilpotent if the so called lower central series terminates at N ∈ N, L(N) = 0,  L(0) = L,  L(n) = [L, L(n−1) ],  ∀n ∈ N.  Abelian Lie algebras are thus 1-step nilpotent. The Lie algebra L is called (N -step) solvable if the so called derived series terminates at N ∈ N, L(N) = 0,  L(0) = L,  L(n) = [L(n−1) , L(n−1) ],  ∀n ∈ N.  A nilpotent Lie algebra is necessarily solvable because L(N) ⊂ L(N) = 0. The nilradical nil(L) ⊂ L of a Lie algebra L is a maximal nilpotent ideal. If it exists then it is unique, because the sum of two nilpotent ideals is again a nilpotent ideal. It always exists when dimF L < ∞. A Lie algebra isomorphism ψ : L 7→ L is called an automorphism of L. Automorphisms of a given Lie algebra comprise a group denoted by Aut(L). A linear operator D ∈ EndF (L) on a Lie algebra L is called a derivation if D[X, Y ] = [DX, Y ] + [X, DY ],  ∀X, Y ∈ L.  The set Der(L) ⊂ EndF (L) of all derivations of a given Lie algebra L comprises a Lie subalgebra  6  of EndF (L) under commutation. Indeed, if D1 , D2 ∈ Der(L) then [D1 , D2 ][X, Y ] = D1 D2 [X, Y ]−D2 D1 [X, Y ] = D1 ([D2 X, Y ]+[X, D2 Y ])−D2 ([D1 X, Y ]+[X, D1 Y ]) = [D1 D2 X, Y ] + [X, D1 D2 Y ] − [D2 D1 X, Y ] − [X, D2 D1 Y ] = [[D1 , D2 ]X, Y ] + [X, [D1 , D2 ]Y ], so that [D1 , D2 ] ∈ Der(L). For every X ∈ L, the operator adX ∈ EndF (L) is a derivation. To see this we will use the Jacobi identity, adX [Y, Z] = [X, [Y, Z]] = −[Z, [X, Y ]] − [Y, [Z, X]] = [[X, Y ], Z] + [Y, [X, Z]] = [adX Y, Z] + [Y, adX Z],  ∀X, Y, Z ∈ L.  Thus the image of the adjoint representation adL ⊂ Der(L) is a Lie subalgebra called the algebra of inner derivations. Those derivations D ∈ Der(L) \ adL are called outer derivations. A linear operator T ∈ EndF (L) on a Lie algebra L is called Lie orthogonal (see [17]) if [TX, TY ] = [X, Y ],  ∀X, Y ∈ L.  Obviously the set O(L) ⊂ EndF (L) of all Lie orthogonal operators on a given Lie algebra L is a monoid under composition with the identity map being the unit. For a Lie algebra L consider the tensor algebra T(L) which is a unital associative graded non-commutative F-algebra, and consider the ideal IL ⊂ T(L) generated by elements of the form X ⊗ Y − Y ⊗ X − [X, Y ] ∈ T(L),  ∀X, Y ∈ L.  The quotient algebra . U(L) = T(L)/IL is called the universal enveloping algebra of L. The natural inclusion L ֒→ T(L) gives rise to an injective map h : L 7→ U(L) such that h(X)h(Y ) − h(Y )h(X) − h([X, Y ]) = 0,  ∀X, Y ∈ L.  Denote by Z(U(L)) = {x ∈ U(L)|  xy = yx,  ∀y ∈ U(L)}  the centre of the universal enveloping algebra. Let D ∈ Der(L) be a derivation, and let U(L) be the universal enveloping algebra. Using  7  the map h : L 7→ U(L) we can consider L as a subspace of U(L). We can extend the action of D to U(L) by requiring it to be a derivation, D(x ∗ y) = Dx ∗ y + x ∗ Dy,  ∀x, y ∈ U(L).  In doing so we start from extending D to entire T(L) as a derivation and using the fact that DIL ⊂ IL which follows from D(X ⊗ Y − Y ⊗ X − [X, Y ]) = DX ⊗ Y − Y ⊗ DX − [DX, Y ] + X ⊗ DY − DY ⊗ X − [X, DY ]. Thus Der(L) ֒→ Der(U(L)).  3  Structure of almost Abelian Lie algebras  In this section we will start serving our main purpose by studying the structure of almost Abelian Lie algebras. Moreover, given a fixed isomorphism ψ0 : L/ ker φ 7→ L′ every other isomorphism ψ : L/ ker φ 7→ L′ can be written as ψ = ψ0 ◦ Ψ where Ψ ∈ Aut(L/ ker φ) is an automorphism. And every automorphism Ψ gives rise to a new isomorphism ψ = ψ0 ◦ Ψ. We conclude in the following remark.
