On Fourier Coefficients of GL(n)-Automorphic Functions over Number Fields  arXiv:1711.11545v2 [math.NT] 6 Mar 2018  Eleftherios Tsiokos Abstract We study Fourier coefficients of GLn (Ak )-automorphic functions φ, for Ak being the adele ring of a number field k. Let FC be an abbreviation for such a Fourier coefficient (and FCs for plural). Roughly speaking, in the present paper we process FCs by iteratively using the operations: Fourier expansions, certain exchanges of Fourier expansions, and conjugations. In Theorem 3.1 we express any FC in terms of—degenerate in many cases—Whittaker FCs. For FCs obtained from the trivial FC by choosing a certain “generic” term in each Fourier expansion involved, we establish a shortcut (Main corollary 6.17) for studying their expressions of the form in Theorem 3.1. The shortcut gives considerably less information, but it remains useful on finding automorphic representations so that for appropriate choices of φ in them, the FC is factorizable and nonzero. Then in Theorems 8.3.11, 8.3.12, and 8.3.18, we study examples of FCs on which this shortcut applies, with many of them turning out to “correspond” to more than one unipotent orbit in GLn . For most of the paper, no knowledge on automorphic forms is necessary. Introduction  We study Fourier coefficients of GLn (Ak )-automorphic functions, for Ak being the adele ring of a number field k. We refer to the results mentioned in the abstract as the three main results of the paper, which are: Theorem 3.1; Main corollary 6.17; and (the similar to each other) Theorems 8.3.11, 8.3.12, and 8.3.18. Other than some modest uses of algebraic geometry, the proofs and statements of these results seem to be very elementary. In particular they do not require1 knowledge in: automorphic forms, representation theory, and number theory. The results of the present paper are introduced in Subsection 1.3. Before it, we motivate the results by discussing Fourier coefficients of automorphic forms, and explaining some notation. Some of these notations lack precision, but they are clarified in Section 2; in particular, the notations in Subsection 1.2 are addressed in Section 2 (mainly in Subsection 2.5) in a clarified, refined, and independent way. Plenty of new notation is used, most of it—A-operations and A-trees (introduced in Subsection 1.2)—is about the standard types of operations we mentioned in the abstract. Let F ∈ Ak,n , and φ be a GLn (Ak )-automorphic function. The main operations we will be applying to F(φ) are the following: (a) Fourier expansions. Consider a unipotent algebraic subgroup V of GLn defined over k, such that: DF ⊇ [V, V ]; vF = F for all v ∈ V (we define vF in (c) below); and F(V ∩ DF ) = 0. Then, the function F(φ) is invariant on V (k)(DF ∩ V )(Ak ), and we can apply to F(φ) the Fourier expansion along V (k)(V ∩ DF )(Ak ) \ V (Ak ). (b) Certain exchanges of Fourier expansions. In the unfolding of Rankin-Selberg integrals, Fourier expansions as in (a) have been used considerably. We apply to F(φ) a special case of such unfoldings, in which the unipotent group occurring in the integration is exchanged by an other unipotent group. For the needs of the present paper, this unfolding has been explained in sufficient generality in [GRS2] in Lemma 7.1. This lemma appears in the present paper as Part 2 of Fact 2.6.1. (c) Conjugations. For γ ∈ GLn (k) we define γF to be the AF with domain γDF γ −1 , given by γF(n) = F(γ −1 nγ) for n ∈ γDF γ −1 . (3) We then have  F(φ)(γ −1 g) = (γF)(φ) for g ∈ GLn (Ak ).  One directly notices that these three operations on F(φ) can be expressed as operations on F without referring to φ (for example, in formula (3) we see this for conjugations). This is how we proceed, and we will be calling any such operation on F an (A, k)-operation. We iteratively apply (A, k)-operations in a way that gives certain directed trees with vertices labeled with AFs, which we call (A, k)-trees, or if k is not specified, A-trees (similarly k is removed or added in other notations). An A-step, say ξ, is an A-tree formed by applying only one A-operation. If this Aoperation corresponds to (a) (resp. (b), (c)) above, we say that ξ is an e-step (resp. ue-step, c-step). We choose the direction of any (A, k)-tree, say Ξ, so that the edges point away from a vertex which we call the input vertex of Ξ; the other external vertices are called output vertices of Ξ (if Ξ is trivial, its input vertex is also called its output vertex). The label of the input (resp. an output) vertex of Ξ is called the input (resp. an output) AF of Ξ. Let F ∈ Ak , S ⊂ Ak , and H be an algebraic group defined over k. By an (F → S, H, k)tree we mean an (A, k)-tree with: input AF equal to F; all its output AFs belonging in S; all its data defined inside H, and each conjugation being by an element in H(k). If we only want to specify a proper subset of {F, S, H, k} we adjust the notation as needed, for example we write “(F, H, k)-tree”. Throughout the current paragraph, Version 2 for both Weak questions 1.1.1 and 1.1.3 is assumed to be chosen. The way each (A, k)-step corresponds to an identity in automorphic forms is given in Fact 2.6.1 (the word “Fact” is reserved for known results). Then Fact 2.6.2 follows which states that for an (F, GLn , k)-tree Ξ the following two hold: 1. The function F(φ) is nonzero if and only if there is an output AF Z in Ξ for which Z(φ) is nonzero. 2. The function F(φ) is factorizable if and only if the next two conditions are true: (i) there is at most one output vertex of Ξ, so that for its label Z we have Z(φ) is nonzero, and (ii) if such a vertex exists, Z(φ) is factorizable. The first main result is Theorem 3.1, in the proof of which, for every AF F ∈ Ak,n , an (F → Ak (Un ), Pn , k)-tree which we call Ξst (F) or Ξst , is inductively defined. This result is based on simple refinements and modifications of unfolding processes that have been used in the study of the GLn × GLm Rankin-Selberg integrals for n 6= m. For example, by appropriately removing from Ξst (F∅,n ) one copy of each among Ξst (F∅,k ) for k < n, the tree obtained roughly corresponds to calculating the Fourier expansion over Un (k) \ Un (Ak ) of a GLn (Ak ) cusp form. We discussed a motivation for constructing an (F → Ak (Un ), GLn , k)-tree in the last two paragraphs of the previous subsection. As in [GK], in the Fourier expansion above, and other later unfoldings, the reasons for using Pn instead of GLn can be seen in the inductive definition of Ξst (F) and in many other constructions involving Ξst (F). In most constructions of A-trees in the present paper, the definition of Ξst (F) is followed considerably (but usually not followed completely for too long within a big A-tree). In my experience, in many of the AFs F ∈ Ak,n I am interested in, understanding (i) in the previous subsection for an (F → Ck,n )-tree by directly constructing it, takes some time (whether or not this A-tree follows closely the definition of Ξst (F)). The second main result of the present paper (Main corollary 6.17) gives a shortcut to these calculations if both the following hold: • Among the questions we stated for F, we restrict to addressing Weak question 1.1.1.(F). • F is obtained as an output-AF of an (F∅,n , GLn )-tree, so that each AF of an e-step of this tree which is encountered in the path (inside this tree) from F∅,n to F, is chosen in a “certain generic way”. By “certain generic way” we mean that: it belongs to an open subset of the variety consisting of the output AFs of the e-step, on which (referring to the open subset) an algebraic subgroup of GLn acts by conjugation freely and transitively (and hence by Zariski’s main theorem this group is isomorphic to this open subset). Such AFs F form a set we call Bn . An example of an AF in Bn , is any AF, say Wn , in A(Un ) which is nontrivial on every positive simple root group (of course the next paragraph is trivial for F being equal to Wn ). The proof that Wn ∈ Bn , is directly discerned in the proof of the Fourier expansion over Un (k) \ Un (A) of GLn (Ak )-cusp forms.  5  We roughly use Main corollary 6.17 as follows: once in the computation of an F-tree (with F ∈ Bn ) a nilpotent orbit a of dimension 2dim (DF ) “occurs”, we obtain from Main corollary 6.17 that this orbit is minimal among the nilpotent orbits “occurring” (in fact of minimal dimension), and that it does not “occur” again; also the orbits in the set Of (F) := {a ∈ O(F) : mult(a, F) < ∞} are exactly the ones in the previous sentence (and hence this set is equal to {a ∈ O(F) : mult(a, F) = 1}). Some thoughts of D. Ginzburg, on a dimension equation he has formulated which is satisfied by many among the familiar Rankin-Selberg integrals, are related to Main corollary 6.17. A short discussion on such relations appears later (from Section 6) in the paper, in Subsection 8.4, after Example 8.4.7. The third main result (Theorems 8.3.11, 8.3.12, and 8.3.18) answers Weak question 1.1.1.(F) (through Version 1), for some choices of F ∈ BN , for a positive integer N . For many of these choices of AFs F, there are more than one orbits positively answering Weak question 1.1.1.(F). For a quick rough idea of the AFs addressed in the third main result see the pictures in Subsection 8.2. Nilpotent and unipotent orbits are in bijective correspondence, and I have chosen to use the former. We therefore define Nn to be the set of nilpotent orbits of the action by conjugation of GLn on Lie(GLn ). Given an orbit a ∈ Nn , for a1 × a1 ,...,am × am being the dimensions of the Jordan blocks of a Jordan matrix belonging to a, we obtain a partition n = a1 +...+am which we frequently identify with a, and write a = [a1 , ..., am ]. The way the first four are used must be quite usual. A claim is used as a lemma but it is stated and proved within the proof of an other statement. An observation is a statement similar to a lemma but very easy (or even trivial) to prove. Some statements have the label “Known” next to their name. This label means that • the statement is known; • even if the statement is followed by any part of a proof it admits, this part of proof is also known. I preferred to avoid the choice between “Theorem” and “Proposition” for the statements with the label “Known”, and hence I use one word for all such cases which is “Fact”. However, the names “Corollary”, “Lemma”, and “Claim” are used with the label “Known”. Most of the observations can be discerned in the literature, but I do not attach to them the label “Known”, due to their low level of difficulty. Of course there are situations the above formalizations would need changes, for example in certain statements in which most of the effort is in the formulation and not in the proof, but at least for the present paper I found them adequate. A square is used as the end of proof sign. If it refers to a proof occurring within an other proof, it is followed by a reminder of what we just proved. For example: Claim, Step i.
