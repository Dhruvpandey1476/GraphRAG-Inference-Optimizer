arXiv:1709.02146v1 [math.RT] 7 Sep 2017  MACKEY ALGEBRAS WHICH ARE GORENSTEIN IVO DELL’AMBROGIO AND JAN ŠŤOVÍČEK Abstract. We complete the picture available in the literature by showing that the integral Mackey algebra is Gorenstein if and only if the group order is square-free, in which case it must have Gorenstein dimension one. We illustrate this result by looking in details at the examples of the cyclic group of order four and the Klein four group.  Let G be a finite group and R a commutative ring of coefficients. The Mackey algebra µR (G), introduced in [TW95], is a finite-rank free R-algebra whose representations form precisely the category of Mackey functors for G with R-coefficients. ` It can be defined as the endomorphism algebra of H≤G G/H in the category of finite G-sets and R-linear combinations of spans of G-maps. In this note we will say that a (unital, associative, non-necessarily commutative) ring S is Gorenstein 1 if its injective dimension over itself as a left module is finite. The latter number n is the Gorenstein dimension of S, and we will sometimes say for short that S is n-Gorenstein. Famously, Gorenstein rings are ubiquitous in mathematics: see [Bas63] for the classical case of local commutative rings, and e.g. [DSŠ17] for a wide-ranging look at non-commutative rings and small categories. The goal of this note is to clarify the literature on the following point: Theorem 1. The integral Mackey algebra µZ (G) is Gorenstein if and only if the order of G is square-free, in which case it has Gorenstein dimension one. Note that this contradicts [Gre92, Lemma 2.2], which states that projective Mackey functors over any finite group have injective dimension one. The lemma in question was used to prove that the the finitistic dimension of µZ (G) equals one, and a different proof of the latter theorem was provided (together with several other similar results) by Bouc, Stancu and Webb; see [BSW17, Theorem 1.2]. Still, the question whether µZ (G) is always Gorenstein was left open. As it turns out, it is Gorenstein precisely for groups G with square-free order. These are also the only groups for which µZ (G) can be made into a symmetric Z-algebra, by a theorem of Rognerud [Rog15] (we will use this result, so more on this below); and they are also precisely the groups for which the Burnside ring B(G) is (1-)Gorenstein or even a symmetric Z-algebra, by theorems of Krämer [Krä74] and Gustafson [Gus77]. Proof of Theorem 2. The main point of the proof was discovered by Gustafson [Gus77]: when |G| is square-free one can define a nice bilinear form βG on the integral Burnside ring B(G) := K0 (G-set, ∐, ×). More precisely: square-free implies solvable ([Hal59, Theorem 9.4.3] or [Rog15, Lemma 5.4]), which implies that there is precisely one conjugacy class of subgoups of G of any given order d dividing |G|. Then the bilinear form βG : B(G) × B(G) → Z is defined by sending (x, y) to [G/1]∗ (x · y), where [G/1]∗ : B(G) → Z is the form sending a basis element [G/H] ∈ B(G) to 1 if H = 1 and to 0 otherwise. The form is symmetric (βG (x, y) = βG (y, x)), non-degenerate (in that it induces and isomorphism ∼ B(G) → B(G)∗ onto the Z-dual) and associative (βG (x, y · z) = βG (x · y, z)). By tensoring with R, we obtain such a form over RB(G) = R ⊗Z B(G) for any ring R. The second ingredient is due to Rognerud [Rog15], who showed that to give an associative symmetric bilinear form β over the Mackey algebra µR (G) amounts to giving a family {βH : RB(H) × RB(H) → R}H≤G of associative symmetric Rbilinear forms indexed by the subgroups of G and satisfying βG (1RB(G) , indG H (x)) = βH (1RB(H) , x). Since the Gustafson forms satisfy this compatibility, they yield a bilinear form on µR (G) which again is symmetric, non-degenerate and associative. (See [Rog15, §3] for more details). We may now immediately conclude with the third and final ingredient, namely the following criterion for the Gorenstein property.  Lemma 3. Let R be an n-Gorenstein commutative ring, and let A be an R-algebra which is finitely generated projective as an R-module. If A admits a symmetric, non-degenerate and associative bilinear form β : A × A → R and if the unit map R → A admits an R-linear retraction, then A is also an n-Gorenstein ring. Proof. This generalises the classical fact that a symmetric Frobenius algebra (a.k.a. symmetric algebra over a field) is self-injective, and is itself a special case of the much more general ‘relative Serre functor’ criterion proved in [DSŠ17, Theorem 1.6]. Indeed, in the notation of loc. cit., we may take C to be A, seen as an R-linear category with a single object whose endomorphism algebra is A. Then the Serre functor S is the identity morphism A → A and the isomorphism σ of left R-modules ∼  σ : A −→ HomR (A, R) is simply the one induced by β, namely x 7→ (y 7→ β(y, x)). The naturality of σ required in loc. cit. means that it is an isomorphism of A-bimodules, and the latter amounts precisely to the associativity and symmetric properties of β.  Proof of Theorem 1. Let us first inspect the corresponding modulo 2 Burnside rings F2 B(C4 ) and F2 B(C2 × C2 ). A general theory in [Gus77] says that given a finite p-group G, Fp B(G) is a local commutative finite dimensional algebra ([Gus77, Lemma 3]). Moreover, the socle of Fp B(G) P contains the element [G/1] and, if |G| > p, also the (different) non-zero element P ≤G,|H|=p [G/H] ([Gus77, pp. 11–14]). Example 5. The underlying group of the integral Burnside ring ZB(G) for G = C4 has rank three and a basis [G/G], [G/H] and [G/1], where 1 < H < G and H is cyclic of order two. The element [G/G] is the unity of the ring. To ease the notation, we write g = [G/1] and h = [G/H]. Then we obtain a ring isomorphism ZB(C4 ) ∼ = Z[g, h]/(g 2 − 4g, h2 − 2h, gh − 2g) by direct computation. The modulo 2 version simplifies to F2 B(C4 ) ∼ = F2 [g, h]/(g 2 , h2 , gh) . Observably soc F2 B(C4 ) = F2 g⊕F2 h is two-dimensional, so this is not a self-injective ring (conclude e.g. with Lemma 7 below, or use some local commutative algebra). Example 6.
